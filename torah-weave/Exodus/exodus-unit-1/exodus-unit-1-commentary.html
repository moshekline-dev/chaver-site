Exodus Unit 1: The People in Egypt, and One Man at the Bush

Who Set You Over Us?

Moses’ first attempt to help his people ends badly. He goes out to his brothers, sees an Egyptian beating a Hebrew, and kills the Egyptian. The next day he finds two Hebrews fighting and tells the one in the wrong to stop. The man turns on him: “Who set you as a man, an officer and a judge over us? Do you mean to kill me, as you killed the Egyptian?” (2:14).

In English the question reads as an ordinary rebuke. In Hebrew it repeats the language of the first chapter, where Egypt “set over them officers of forced labour, to afflict them with their burdens” (1:11). Both verses use the same verb, sim (“to set”), and the same title, sar (“officer”). The words that described Egypt’s rule over the people are now applied to Moses.

This is not the only such repetition. When Moses goes out to his brothers, he “saw their burdens” (2:11), using the same word for the burdens that Egypt laid on the people in 1:11 (be-sivlotam). The book opens with the sons of Israel who came to Egypt, “each man and his house” (1:1). The story of Moses opens with “a man of the house of Levi” (2:1). Phrases used of the whole people in the first chapter are used of one man in the second, and, as we will see, the pattern continues through the unit.

These repetitions belong to a larger puzzle. Exodus begins with a list: “These are the names of the sons of Israel who came to Egypt with Jacob.” A book that opens with the names of a people tells us that the people are its subject. Yet after one chapter the unit turns to a single person. We follow Moses from his birth to his flight from Egypt, his marriage in Midian, and a long conversation at a burning bush. The people appear only once more, in three verses, when they groan under their labour and their cry goes up to the deity (2:23–25).

Why does a book about a people begin with the story of one man? The usual answer is that the people will need a leader to bring them out of Egypt, and so the leader must first be introduced. That is true, but it leaves a second question: why is the story of Moses told in words first used of the people?

We will suggest that the unit answers both questions through its arrangement. In this book the deity will speak to one man, and that man will carry back to the people what he hears. Before that can happen, he must be shown to be the people’s equivalent, one who can stand in their place. The unit shows this by setting the people and Moses side by side, row by row, and describing them in the same terms.

The Unit’s Boundaries

The list that opens the book goes on through Reuben, Simeon, Levi and the rest (1:2–4). Near the end of the next unit the list is begun again: “These are the heads of their fathers’ houses” (6:14). Reuben comes first, then Simeon, then Levi, and at Levi the list stops running through the sons and follows one line from father to son until it reaches Aaron and Moses: “It was this Aaron and this Moses to whom YHWH said, Bring the children of Israel out of the land of Egypt” (6:26). The two lists enclose Units 1 and 2, which make a pair. The first list names a people; the second ends with the two men who will bring it out.

Within the pair, Unit 1 ends with Jethro’s “Go in peace” (4:18). The line could have been drawn where Moses flees, or where he comes to the bush. The unit has three rows, as the table below shows, and in each of them the people stand beside Moses; both the flight and the bush fall inside that design. The last verse also closes what the story of Moses opened: when he had grown up he went out “to his brothers” (2:11) and then fled to Midian (2:15); now Midian gives him leave, and he asks to go back to “my brothers who are in Egypt” (4:18). The next unit opens by naming the place: “YHWH said to Moses in Midian, Go, return to Egypt” (4:19).

The Unit’s Architecture

The unit is laid out in two columns and three rows. Column A tells the story of the people, and Column B the story of Moses. The table can be read in two directions. Reading down a column, we follow either the people or Moses through three stages. Reading across a row, we see the two at the same stage, side by side. Rows 1 and 3 are each divided into smaller sections, marked a, b and c (and d in Row 3), and each section in Column A stands opposite a section in Column B. Row 2 is not divided.

The arrangement is the one Genesis opens with. The six days of creation also stand in two columns, the first three days making places and the last three filling them, among them creatures that “swarm” and “multiply.” Here the order is reversed, the many coming first and Moses second, and the creation’s words are applied to the people, who “were fruitful and swarmed and multiplied … and the land was filled with them” (1:7).

We begin with Row 1, where the parallel between the two columns is drawn most closely.

The Rows Read Across

Row 1: Before Revelation

In Row 1 neither YHWH nor Elohim speaks to anyone. Elohim acts once, in Column A, for the midwives who “feared the deity” (1:17, 20), and no one in the story is told of it. The row is long, and each column is divided into three sections. In each pair, the section on Moses takes up the matter of the section on the people opposite it: first houses, then Egypt’s oppression, then sons and daughters. What is told of the whole people in Column A is told again of Moses in Column B, often in the same words.

The first pair concerns houses. The people come to Egypt “each man and his house” (1Aa, 1:1). Moses’ story begins when “a man of the house of Levi went and took a daughter of Levi” (1Ba, 2:1). The people enter the book as a group of households; Moses enters it through the founding of one household.

The second pair concerns Egypt and the people. The new king fears the Hebrews because they are “many and mightier than we” (1:9), and he fears what they might do: “if a war comes, they will join our enemies and fight against us” (1Ab, 1:10). So he sets “officers of forced labour” over them, “to afflict them with their burdens” (1:11). In 1Bb, Moses goes out to his brothers and “saw their burdens” (2:11). He sees an Egyptian beating a Hebrew and “struck the Egyptian” (2:12). The next day a Hebrew asks him, “Who set you as a man, an officer and a judge over us?” (2:14). The words of 1Ab return in 1Bb. Moses sees the burdens that Egypt laid on the people. He strikes an Egyptian, the act the king feared from the people as a whole. And he is taken for one more officer set over them.

When Pharaoh hears what Moses has done, he “sought to kill Moses” (2:15). This is the king whose daughter drew Moses out of the water and took him as her son: “he became her son” (2:10). Moses has been raised in the court as one of the royal family, and now the head of that house seeks his death, as he had sought the death of the Hebrew sons. Within two verses Moses is refused by his brothers and hunted by the house that raised him.

The third pair concerns sons and daughters. The king tells the midwives of the Hebrews, “If it is a son, kill him; and if it is a daughter, she shall live” (1Ac, 1:16). When the midwives do not obey, Pharaoh orders all his people to throw every son that is born into the Nile (1:22). In 1Bc, Moses, a Hebrew son born under that decree and now sought by Pharaoh himself, has fled to Midian. There he helps the seven daughters of the priest of Midian at a well (2:16–17), marries one of them, Zipporah, and “she bore a son” (2:22). By fleeing, Moses has saved not only himself but his descendants: his son is born outside Egypt, beyond the reach of the decree. Row 1 thus ends with the people’s sons still under a sentence of death in Egypt, and with the son of Moses alive in a foreign land.

In both columns it is women who keep the sons alive. In Column A the midwives defy the king: they “feared the deity, and did not do as the king of Egypt had spoken to them, and let the children live” (1:17), the boys whom the king had ordered killed. In Column B the daughters whom the decree spares do the same for Moses. His mother, “a daughter of Levi,” bears him and hides him. His sister watches over the basket. Pharaoh’s own daughter “had pity on him,” though she knew him for “one of the Hebrews’ children,” and took him as her son (2:6, 10). And in Midian it is through the seven daughters of the priest that the fugitive comes to their father’s house (2:19–21), where he is given a wife and where his son is born.

The row ends with a name and the reason given for it: “He called his name Gershom, for he said, I have been a stranger in a foreign land” (2:22). The name joins two words, ger, “stranger,” and sham, “there”: Gershom, “a stranger there.” We have just seen why. Moses has no place left, either among his brothers or in the house of Pharaoh. At the close of Row 1 the people have a name but no one to speak for them, and the one man whose life has been told in their words calls himself a stranger.

Row 2: The Beginning of Revelation

Row 2 is short, three verses in Column A and five in Column B, and it is not divided into sections. In it YHWH enters the unit, and both YHWH and Elohim come to Moses’ column. In the people’s column a cry goes up to the deity, and in Moses’ column a call comes to him.

In 2A the king of Egypt dies, and “the children of Israel groaned from the labour, and cried out, and their cry went up to the deity from the labour (min ha-avodah)” (2:23). In 2B Moses comes to “the mountain of the deity, to Horeb” (3:1), and a voice speaks to him: “Elohim called to him from the midst of the bush (mi-tokh ha-sneh)” (3:4). In each column there is one voice, and each voice comes out of something. The people’s cry comes up out of their labour. That is the word of the first row of their column, where Egypt “made their lives bitter with hard labour” (1:14), and here it comes twice in a single verse. The call to Moses comes out of a bush that “was burning with fire, and the bush was not consumed” (3:2). The cry goes up from the people to the deity, and the call comes from the deity to Moses.

Both sections then turn on seeing. In 2A, “Elohim saw the children of Israel, and Elohim knew” (2:25). In 2B, “YHWH saw that he turned aside to see” (3:4). The verb is the same on both sides (va-yar, “saw”), and it stands a third time two verses earlier, written with the same letters: “the messenger of YHWH appeared (va-yera) to him in a flame of fire” (3:2), that is, was seen by him. What is seen differs. In 2A Elohim sees the children of Israel as a whole, and nothing more is said. In 2B Moses is seen as one man who does something: he sees the bush, says “Let me turn aside and see this great sight” (3:3), and is seen turning aside. Then he is called by name, “Moses, Moses,” and he answers, “Here I am” (3:4). The people are seen. Moses sees, is seen, and answers.

In 2A only Elohim is named. He hears the people’s groaning, remembers “his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac and with Jacob” (2:24), sees and knows, and none of this is told to anyone in the story. In 2B both names stand in one verse: “YHWH saw that he turned aside to see, and Elohim called to him” (3:4). In Row 1 neither YHWH nor Elohim was named in Moses’ column. Here both come to him. The cry of the people has been heard, and the answer to it is given to Moses.

The first command spoken to him is a limit: “Do not come near (al tikrav) here. Take your sandals off your feet, for the place on which you stand is holy ground (admat kodesh)” (3:5). Since Elohim made the seventh day holy (Gen 2:3), the Torah has called nothing holy. The word comes back here, at the bush, said to Moses alone, and the place is “the mountain of the deity.” In Row 3 Moses is told that the people will come to this mountain too.

Row 3: The Revelation

In Row 3 the deity speaks at length, first to Moses about the people (Column A), then with Moses about Moses himself (Column B). The row is divided into four sections on each side, and each section in Column B answers the section opposite it in Column A. In each pair Column A speaks of the people and Column B of Moses, and the same words stand on both sides. In each of the first three pairs what is promised for the people is met by a doubt of Moses about himself, and he is answered: they will not hear him; he has no words; let someone else be sent. In the fourth he goes.

The first pair is about being seen and being heard. In 3Aa YHWH says, “I have surely seen the affliction of my people who are in Egypt, and their cry I have heard (shama’ti)” (3:7). In 3Ba Moses answers with a fear: “They will not believe me, and they will not hear (yishme’u) my voice, for they will say, YHWH has not appeared to you” (4:1). The verbs are the same on both sides. YHWH has seen the people and heard them; Moses is afraid that the people will not hear him and will not believe that YHWH has been seen by him. Between the two verses, in Column A, Moses has already been told the opposite: “They will hear your voice (ve-shame’u le-kolekha)” (3:18). Moses does not take the word for it.

The same pair turns on a hand. In 3Aa YHWH has come down “to rescue it from the hand of Egypt” (3:8). In 3Ba he asks Moses, “What is that in your hand?” and Moses answers, “A staff” (4:2). The people are to be taken out of one hand, and the means is put into another.

The pair goes on with the rest of the same verse. YHWH has come down to rescue the people from the hand of Egypt “and to bring it up from that land (min ha-aretz)” (3:8). In 3Ba, when Moses has said what is in his hand, he is told, “Throw it to the ground (artzah)” (4:3). The word on both sides is eretz, land or ground. The people are to be brought up from it, and the staff is thrown down to it.

Column A says outright how the people will be brought up. YHWH has come down, and two verses later he says to Moses, “And now go, and I will send you to Pharaoh, and bring my people, the children of Israel, out of Egypt” (3:10). What YHWH has come down to do, he sends Moses to do. Column B shows this in the staff. It is in Moses’ hand, and at YHWH’s word it goes down to the ground: “He threw it to the ground, and it became a serpent, and Moses fled from before it” (4:3). Then he is told, “Send out your hand and take hold of its tail,” and “he sent out his hand and seized it, and it became a staff in his palm” (4:4). Moses has drawn back once already in this pair. At the bush he “hid his face, for he was afraid to look at the deity” (3:6). Now he flees from what he himself has thrown down, and at YHWH’s word he takes it up again. This is the staff that he is told, when his objections are over, to take in his hand (4:17). YHWH has come down to bring the people up from that land, and he will do it by the hand of the man he sends, with this staff in it.

Each section ends with a sign, and the two signs are of different kinds. In 3Aa Moses asks, “Who am I, that I should go to Pharaoh, and that I should bring the children of Israel out of Egypt?” (3:11). The answer is, “For I will be with you (ki ehyeh immakh), and this is the sign (ha-ot) for you that I have sent you: when you bring the people out of Egypt, you shall serve the deity on this mountain” (3:12). This sign cannot be seen in advance. It will be seen only when the people have come out of Egypt and stand at the mountain where Moses is standing now. In 3Ba the signs are for the present, and they are given “so that they may believe that YHWH, the deity of their fathers … has appeared to you” (4:5): the staff that becomes a serpent, the hand that comes out of his bosom leprous as snow and is restored, and the water of the Nile that becomes blood on the dry land. The section ends, “If they will not believe even these two signs (ha-otot)” (4:9). The people’s sign tells Moses where they are going. Moses’ signs are to make the people believe him now.

The second pair opens the same way on both sides, with Moses speaking: “Moses said to the deity” (3:13) in Column A, and “Moses said to YHWH” (4:10) in Column B. Before he has been given the name, Moses speaks to “the deity”; after it, he speaks to YHWH. In 3Ab Moses asks what he is to say when the people ask him, “What is his name?” (3:13). In 3Bb he says that he cannot speak at all: “I am not a man of words … for I am heavy of mouth and heavy of tongue” (4:10). The people will need a name, and Moses has no words. The answers to both are read below, in “The Two Names and the Mouth.”

The third pair is about sending, and a hand again. In 3Ac Moses is told how the people will come out. The elders will listen, Pharaoh will not, “and I will send out my hand and strike Egypt with all my wonders that I will do in its midst, and after that he will send you” (3:20). In 3Bc Moses answers, “Please, my Lord, send, please, by the hand of whom you will send” (4:13). The deity will send his hand against Egypt, and Pharaoh will send the people out. Moses asks that the deity send someone else.

“The anger of YHWH burned against Moses” (4:14), and Moses is given his brother. Aaron “can surely speak,” and he is already on his way to meet him. Moses is to put the words in Aaron’s mouth, and YHWH will be “with your mouth and with his mouth” (4:15). Then the section says what each of them will be: “He shall speak for you to the people, and he shall be to you for a mouth, and you shall be to him for a deity (le-elohim)” (4:16). The section closes with the staff: “And this staff you shall take in your hand, with which you shall do the signs” (4:17).

The last pair is about going. In 3Ad the people are promised: “When you go (telekhun), you shall not go (telkhu) empty-handed” (3:21). Each woman will ask her neighbour for “vessels of silver and vessels of gold, and clothing,” and they will put them on their sons and their daughters, “and you shall strip Egypt” (3:22). In 3Bd Moses goes (va-yelekh) back to Jethro his father-in-law and asks his leave: “Let me go, please, and return to my brothers who are in Egypt, and see whether they are still alive.” Jethro answers, “Go (lekh) in peace” (4:18). The people will go out of Egypt, and they will take its silver and gold with them. Moses asks to go back into Egypt, and he is sent in peace.

The last verse of the unit takes up the first row of Moses’ column. There he went out to “his brothers” and saw their burdens (2:11), and he fled from Egypt to Midian (2:15). Here he asks to go back to “my brothers who are in Egypt.” The man who called himself a stranger in a foreign land (2:22) now asks to go back to the people from whom he was set apart, with what he has been given at the bush.

The Columns Read Down

Column A: The People

Read from top to bottom, Column A describes what happens to the people, and in it they are almost entirely passive. In Row 1 Egypt sets officers over them and orders their sons thrown into the Nile. In Row 2 their groaning is heard. In Row 3 Moses is told how they will be brought out of Egypt and into “a land flowing with milk and honey” (3:8). By the end of the column the people’s redemption has been fully described, although none of it has yet taken place.

The rows describe Moses in the people’s terms. The columns keep him apart from them. In Column A the people are called by their name, “the sons of Israel,” from the first verse on. In Column B that name never appears. There they are “the Hebrews” (2:6, 7, 11, 13), “his brothers” (2:11) and, in the last verse, “my brothers” (4:18).

Column B: Moses

Column B describes Moses, and here the subject is active. He kills the Egyptian, flees, waters the flock of the priest of Midian, and turns aside to look at the bush. In Row 3 he raises one objection after another and is given an answer to each: signs to show the people, a promise that YHWH will be with his mouth, his brother Aaron to speak for him, and a staff with which to perform the signs. At the top of the column neither YHWH nor Elohim has spoken to him. At the bottom he has been sent.

Moses himself is called a “man” (ish). He sees “an Egyptian man beating a Hebrew man” and looks around to see “that there was no man” (2:11–12). The Hebrew asks who set him “as a man, an officer and a judge” (2:14). The daughters of Midian tell their father, “An Egyptian man rescued us from the hand of the shepherds” (2:19). In Row 1 Moses is a man among men. In Row 3 he says, “I am not a man of words” (4:10), and he is told, “you shall be to him for a deity” (4:16).

Who appears to Moses changes down his column. In Row 1 neither YHWH nor Elohim is named there; in Row 2 both come to him at the bush; in Row 3 only YHWH speaks to him, and the one time elohim stands on its own in the column, it is said of Moses himself. At the end of his column he stands toward Aaron as the deity stands toward him. He has not reached this place by his own wish. It is given to him after he has asked to be let off, and while YHWH is angry with him.

The Vertical Marks

The marks that run down the columns all stand in Row 1, where each column has three sections. In Column A they follow the people’s numbers. The first section ends, “The children of Israel were fruitful and swarmed and multiplied and became very, very mighty (va-yirbu va-ya’atzmu)” (1Aa, 1:7). In the second the king says, “the people of the children of Israel are many and mightier than we (rav ve-atzum)” (1Ab, 1:9), and the same section says what comes of his measures: “the more they afflicted them, the more they multiplied (yirbeh) and the more they spread” (1:12). In the third, after the midwives have let the boys live, “the people multiplied and became very mighty (va-yirev ha-am va-ya’atzmu)” (1Ac, 1:20). The people’s numbers are the reason the king gives for the oppression, and they go on growing through the forced labour and through the order given to the midwives.

Column B describes growth too, though not in the same words. “The child grew up (va-yigdal)” (1Ba, 2:10), and a verse later “Moses grew up (va-yigdal)” (1Bb, 2:11). What is said of the people’s numbers is said of the boy’s years.

The other mark in Column B joins two names. Pharaoh’s daughter “called his name Moses, and said, For from the water I drew him” (1Ba, 2:10); Moses “called his name Gershom, for he said, I have been a stranger in a foreign land” (1Bc, 2:22), the verse on which Row 1 ended. The two namings in his column, Moses by the princess and Gershom by Moses, are each given with a reason, and the table marks them as a pair down the column, one at the end of 1Ba and the other at the end of 1Bc.

The unit opens with the names of a people. It gives Moses a name from the water and his son a name for being a stranger, and in Row 3 it gives the deity’s names.

The Two Names and the Mouth

Elohim answers the question of the name (3Ab, 3:13) twice. The first answer gives the name Ehyeh: “Thus shall you say to the children of Israel, Ehyeh has sent me to you” (3:14). The second gives the name YHWH: “Thus shall you say to the children of Israel, YHWH, the deity of your fathers, the deity of Abraham, the deity of Isaac and the deity of Jacob, has sent me to you” (3:15). Then come two sentences: “This is my name for ever, and this is my remembrance from generation to generation” (zeh shemi le-olam ve-zeh zikhri le-dor dor). Two names are given, and two sentences follow, each beginning “this.” The first sentence goes with the first name, and the second with the second:

Read straight through, the verses give two names. Read down the columns, they say what distinguishes them. The first column is Elohim’s own. Elohim speaks, names himself Ehyeh, and that is “my name for ever (le-olam).” It comes with no fathers and no generations. Elohim is the deity outside time, something like the deity of the philosophers. In the second column Elohim gives a second name, YHWH, and that is “my remembrance from generation to generation (le-dor dor).” YHWH is named together with the fathers, Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, one by one. He is the personal deity, known to persons and remembered in history from one generation to the next. Both answers are spoken by one speaker, and both end in the same words, “has sent me to you.” The one who speaks gives two names, one outside time and one within history.

Moses’ objection about his mouth is answered with the word of the first name. YHWH tells him, “Now go, and I will be (ehyeh) with your mouth, and I will teach you what you shall speak” (4:12). The word ehyeh, “I will be,” is found in Exodus only in this unit: at the sign of the mountain, “I will be with you” (3:12); three times in the name (3:14); and twice of Moses’ mouth (4:12, 15). The name for ever, given to Moses for the people, is also the promise to be with the mouth that will carry it to them, and both names are his to bring back.

Two Strangers

At the end of Row 1 Moses calls himself a stranger, and in Row 3 it becomes clear that he is not the only one. When Moses pictures himself coming to the people with his message, he expects to be asked about two persons. The first is the deity: “they will say to me, What is his name?” (3:13). The second is Moses himself: “they will not believe me … for they will say, YHWH has not appeared to you” (4:1). Moses expects to be asked the name of the deity of their fathers, and one whose name has to be asked is a stranger. In Genesis YHWH speaks for the last time to Jacob in Laban’s house: “Return to the land of your fathers and to your birthplace, and I will be with you (ve-ehyeh immakh)” (Gen 31:3). From then on his voice is not heard until the bush, where Moses is told the same words, “I will be with you” (3:12). YHWH has become a stranger to the people, as Moses has become a stranger to them.

Moses is a stranger even to his own people. He was raised in Pharaoh’s house as the son of Pharaoh’s daughter (2:10). The Hebrew he tried to stop refused him (2:14), the daughters of Midian took him for an Egyptian (2:19), and he named his son Gershom for what he had become (2:22).

The signs answer both questions at once. They are given “so that they may believe that YHWH, the deity of their fathers, the deity of Abraham, the deity of Isaac and the deity of Jacob, has appeared to you” (4:5). One act of belief takes in both strangers: the deity whose name they will now be told, and the man to whom he appeared. When the signs are done before the people, “the people believed” (4:31), and after the sea, “they believed in YHWH and in Moses his servant” (14:31). The two strangers are believed in together.

The Unit in the Book

Exodus is built of nineteen units. Three of them stand alone: the sea, the covenant and the tablets. The other sixteen fall into four quarters of four units each, and the first quarter is Egypt, Units 1 to 4. Unit 1 opens the first quarter and the book. Laid out as a map, the four quarters stand in a square, with the sea, the covenant and the tablets down the middle, as on the small map at the head of this commentary (Figure 1), and here with the names of the units:

Unit 1 stands at the top left. Its place in the book explains a good deal of what we have read in it.

What Moses Is Given, All the People Are Given

The first and the last units of Egypt answer each other. In Unit 1 Moses strikes an Egyptian (2:12), and for that he has to run: “Pharaoh sought to kill Moses, and Moses fled from before Pharaoh” (2:15). In Unit 4 the people leave after Egyptians have been struck again, and this time the blow falls on every house: “at midnight YHWH struck every firstborn in the land of Egypt” (12:29), and Pharaoh himself sends them out (12:31). Moses leaves Egypt twice, first alone and then with all the people.

What Moses is given at the bush, every Israelite is given on the night of the going out:

Moses alone on holy ground: “the place on which you stand is holy ground” (3:5) A holy assembly for the whole people, on the first day and on the seventh (12:16)
The staff for Moses’ hand, “with which you shall do the signs (ha-otot)” (4:17) “It shall be a sign (le-ot) for you on your hand” (13:9)
“I will be with your mouth” (4:12) “So that the teaching of YHWH may be in your mouth” (13:9)

After the bush the word “holy” is not heard again through the return to Egypt and nine plagues. It comes back on the night of the going out, and it no longer describes the ground under one man’s feet but days that the whole people keeps.

The two units between are the way from the one to the other, and Moses is told them in advance at the bush: “I know that the king of Egypt will not let you go … and I will send out my hand and strike Egypt with all my wonders … and after that he will send you” (3:19–20). The refusal is Unit 2: “I do not know YHWH, and I will not send Israel” (5:2). The wonders are Unit 3, which ends with the promise given again: “after that he will send you from here” (11:1). In Unit 4 it is kept, and Egypt presses the people “to send them out of the land in haste” (12:33). The verb is the same throughout (shalah): first the hand is sent out against Egypt, and then Pharaoh at last sends the people. In Unit 1 Moses is also told that the elders “will hear your voice” (3:18). In Unit 2 Aaron speaks the words to them, and “the people believed” (4:31), though not for long (6:9).

The sign of the mountain reaches beyond Egypt. At the bush Moses alone is told, “When you bring the people out of Egypt, you shall serve the deity on this mountain” (3:12). The top row of the map keeps coming back to this one mountain. Aaron meets Moses there (4:27), and on the road the water comes out of the rock “at Horeb” (17:6). The return comes in Unit 7, at the other end of the top row. The people camp “facing the mountain” (19:2), and “all the people answered together and said, All that YHWH has spoken we will do” (19:8). The serving comes in Unit 10, the covenant, when Moses builds an altar beneath the mountain and burnt offerings are brought up to YHWH (24:4–5).

The First Corner

Unit 1 is the top left corner of the map. At each corner of the map there is a holy place with a line drawn around it. At the bush Moses is told, “Do not come near” (3:5). At the mountain, at the other top corner, the line is drawn around the whole people: “whoever touches the mountain shall surely be put to death” (19:12). At the altar, at the lower left, “whoever touches the altar shall become holy” (29:37). At the lower right, the end of the book, “Moses could not come into the tent of meeting, for the cloud dwelt on it, and the glory of YHWH filled the tabernacle” (40:35). The book opens and closes with Moses stopped at a line. (The corners of the map of Genesis are likewise places where a person must not be touched. “Do not come near” is the verb used of Abimelech, who “had not come near” Sarah, Gen 20:4.)

Unit 1 also stands at the head of the first long column of the map, and Unit 19 at the foot of the last. In every unit of the first column YHWH is with Moses and the people are elsewhere (through the plagues they are in Goshen, 9:26), although Unit 1 begins with the whole people before it narrows to Moses. Every unit of the last column is about the people as a whole. At the bush Moses sees “a flame of fire (esh)” (3:2). At the head of the last column, in Unit 7, “Mount Sinai was all smoke, because YHWH had come down on it in fire (ba-esh)” (19:18), “in the sight of all the people” (19:11). At its foot the fire is in the cloud over the tabernacle by night, “in the sight of all the house of Israel” (40:38). Moses alone sees the first fire, and the whole people sees the last.

Into Leviticus

Exodus, Leviticus and Numbers are laid out one beneath the other, and the first column of Exodus runs on into the units of Leviticus in which the priests begin their service. In the same group of units stands the law of a woman who gives birth (Lev 12:2). Unit 1 is full of births: the midwives at the births of Israel’s children (1:15–19), and then Moses’ own, “the woman conceived and bore (va-teled) a son” (2:2). Leviticus has the same verb for bearing (ve-yaledah, Lev 12:2).

The closer tie is the line drawn around what is holy. In Leviticus two of Aaron’s sons bring fire that no one had commanded, and fire comes out and consumes them. Moses tells Aaron what YHWH had said: “Through those near to me I will be made holy” (Lev 10:3). At the head of the column the first command at the bush was “Do not come near” (3:5), and Moses “hid his face, for he was afraid to look at the deity” (3:6). Two men come near, and die of it.

Reading the Unit

We began with the question a Hebrew threw at Moses: “Who set (sam) you as a man, an officer and a judge over us?” (2:14). In Row 1 it has no answer. Egypt had set officers over the people (1:11), and Moses, who had just struck an Egyptian, was taken for one more.

The unit asks “who” twice more. At the bush Moses asks, “Who am I, that I should go to Pharaoh, and that I should bring the children of Israel out of Egypt?” (3:11). The answer does not say who Moses is. It says, “I will be with you” (3:12). When Moses says that he is not a man of words, YHWH answers him with the verb of the Hebrew’s question: “Who set (sam) a mouth for man, or who sets one mute or deaf or seeing or blind? Is it not I, YHWH?” (4:11). Then he tells Moses to set (ve-samta) the words in Aaron’s mouth (4:15). The question of 2:14 is answered at the bush. Moses is not set over the people by Egypt, and not by himself. He is sent by YHWH, who sets the mouth and will be with it.

We also asked why a book that opens with the names of the sons of Israel gives most of its first unit to one man. The deity speaks to one man, and that man carries back to the people what he hears. Each answer at the bush ends, “has sent me to you,” and each is given to Moses to say: “Thus shall you say to the children of Israel” (3:14, 15). To stand in that place, Moses has first to be shown as the people’s equivalent, and the unit shows it by telling his life in their words, row by row. In Row 1 he is a man among men, to whom neither YHWH nor Elohim has yet spoken, a man of the house of Levi who sees their burdens and is taken for one more officer over them. In Row 2 the cry of the people goes up and the call comes down to him. In Row 3 he is given the names, the signs and the staff, and at the end of his column he is told, “you shall be to him for a deity” (4:16). Near its end the Torah calls him “Moses, the man of the deity” (Deut 33:1).

The unit’s place in the book shows the other half. What Moses is given at the bush, every Israelite is given on the night of the going out: holy ground becomes holy days, the sign in his hand becomes a sign on every hand, and the promise to his mouth becomes the teaching in every mouth. He leaves Egypt after striking an Egyptian, and the people leave after the firstborn are struck. The fire that Moses sees alone at the bush, the whole people sees on the mountain. Moses is told in the people’s words, and then the people are given what he was given and repeat his movements.

Moses, told in the people’s words, is also set apart from them, a stranger to his own people, as YHWH has become a stranger to them. The unit ends with both strangers about to be made known together: Moses asks to go back to “my brothers who are in Egypt” (4:18), carrying the names and the commission he has received at the bush.

For Further Investigation: The Serpent and the Garden

What follows is offered as reasoned speculation. It does not rest on the marks in the table of the unit, as the rest of this commentary does, and it reaches back into Genesis.

The first sign given to Moses is a serpent (nachash). As a creature in the story, the serpent has been seen once before in the Torah, in the garden (Gen 3:1–14). Between the garden and the bush the word comes only as a figure of speech, in Jacob’s words about Dan (Gen 49:17). In Exodus the serpent is seen only here, and the one later mention looks back to this moment: “the staff that turned into a serpent” (7:15). The signs of Exodus are written for those who read of them as much as for those who saw them: “so that you may tell in the ears of your son and your son’s son … my signs that I set among them” (10:2). A reader who meets a serpent at the bush has met one before, and it is hard to think that the author put it here without meaning the reader to remember. We have already seen one word come back at the bush from the beginning of Genesis: “holy,” not heard since the seventh day (Gen 2:3). The serpent comes from the story that follows it.

In the garden the serpent is introduced by one quality: it “was more shrewd (arum) than any beast of the field” (Gen 3:1). It speaks to the woman about a tree that she has been told to keep from, “lest you die” (Gen 3:3), and when it has spoken she sees that the tree is “desirable for making wise (le-haskil)” (Gen 3:6). In Exodus the new king’s first counsel to his people is, “Come, let us deal wisely with it (nitchakmah lo), lest it multiply” (1:10). The Hebrew words are different, but the act is of the same kind, and here too it leads to death: the king’s wisdom ends in the order to kill the sons. Like the serpent, the king speaks to women, and he tells them who is to die and who is to live (1:16). The woman in the garden did as the serpent said. The midwives “feared the deity, and did not do as the king of Egypt had spoken to them” (1:17). The woman’s sentence was about birth: “I will greatly multiply (harbah arbeh) your pain … in pain you shall bear sons” (Gen 3:16). The king acts “lest it multiply (pen yirbeh),” and he acts against the births. On this reading the king of Row 1 is acting as the serpent acted.

Moses’ own column echoes the same verse of the garden. There “the woman saw that the tree was good (va-tere ha-ishah ki tov) … and she took (va-tikach) of its fruit” (Gen 3:6). In 1Ba “the woman conceived and bore a son, and she saw him, that he was good (va-tere oto ki tov hu),” and when she could hide him no longer “she took (va-tikach) for him an ark of reeds” (2:2–3). In Genesis no other woman sees that something is good. In the garden the woman takes what she was told would bring death. Here the woman takes an ark, and her son lives.

If the king is acting as the serpent acted, the first sign can be read together with Row 1. Moses has fled once already: “Moses fled from before Pharaoh” (2:15). At the bush the staff becomes a serpent, “and Moses fled from before it” (4:3). These are the only two flights in the unit. The word of command is also the king’s. His last decree was “every son that is born you shall throw (tashlikhuhu) into the Nile” (1:22), and Moses is told, “Throw it (hashlikhehu) to the ground” (4:3). Then he is told to send out his hand and take hold of the serpent, and it is a staff in his palm (4:4). When he is sent to meet Pharaoh at the Nile, he is to take with him “the staff that turned into a serpent” (7:15). The sign may be showing that the man who fled from the king will have hold of the king’s shrewdness, as he has hold of the serpent.

Two more points of contact may belong here. The garden closes with the man kept from the tree of life, “lest he send out his hand and take” (Gen 3:22). He is sent out, and a flame guards the way (Gen 3:23–24). At the bush there is a flame of fire (3:2) and ground that Moses may not come near (3:5); he is sent (3:10), and he is told, “Send out your hand and take hold” (4:4). And the serpent’s promise was “you shall be as elohim” (Gen 3:5). At the end of his column Moses is told, “you shall be to him for a deity (le-elohim)” (4:16).

None of this is marked in the table of the unit, and a full reading would have to set the unit beside the story of the garden as a whole, and not beside single verses. We offer it as a direction for further study.
