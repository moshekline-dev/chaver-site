On the Way, at the Lodging Place

Moses goes back to Egypt because YHWH sends him. “Go, return to Egypt,” YHWH says to him in Midian, “for all the men who sought your life are dead” (4:19). Moses takes his wife and his sons and sets out. Five verses later we read: “On the way, at the lodging place, YHWH met him and sought to put him to death” (4:24).

This may be the most difficult verse in the Torah. Moses can go because the men who sought (ha-mevakshim) his life are dead, and now YHWH, who sent him, seeks (va-yevakkesh) a death. No reason is given. The verse does not even say whose death. It says “him,” and that may be Moses, or it may be his son, for it is the son who is then cut: “Zipporah took a flint and cut off her son’s foreskin, and touched it to his feet, and said, For you are a bridegroom of blood to me. And he let him go. Then she said, A bridegroom of blood, for the circumcision” (4:25–26). The verses do not say whose feet she touched, or to whom she said “you.”

Three verses later the verb “met him” comes again. YHWH tells Aaron, “Go to meet Moses, to the wilderness,” and Aaron “went and met him (va-yifgeshehu) at the mountain of the deity, and kissed him” (4:27). Exodus has the word in these two verses only. On the way back to Egypt there are two meetings, and YHWH is behind both. In the first YHWH himself meets someone and seeks a death. In the second Aaron meets Moses and kisses him.

This is not the only death in the passage. Between the sending and the lodging place Moses is given the message he is to bring to Pharaoh: “Thus says YHWH: Israel is my son, my firstborn. I have said to you, Send my son, that he may serve me, and you have refused to send him; behold, I kill your son, your firstborn” (4:22–23). The next words are “On the way, at the lodging place.” In eight verses the men who sought Moses’ life have died, the death of Pharaoh’s firstborn son has been announced, and YHWH has sought to put someone to death while a son was circumcised.

The usual answer is that Moses’ son had not been circumcised. Abraham was told, “An uncircumcised male who is not circumcised in the flesh of his foreskin, that soul shall be cut off from his people; he has broken my covenant” (Gen 17:14), and at the lodging place the danger passes when Zipporah “cut off” the foreskin. The verb is the same (karat). The answer is likely to be right as far as it goes. It says what turned the death aside. It does not say why the penalty should fall here, on the way that YHWH has just sent Moses, or why YHWH himself is the one who meets him, or why the scene is told so that we cannot say who was in danger. Nothing in the three verses names Abraham, or a covenant, or a fault.

We will not explain these verses away, and we may not be able to explain them at all. What can be shown is how much of the unit is tied to them. When Moses and Aaron stand before Pharaoh for the first time, they ask to go “a way (derekh) of three days in the wilderness” and sacrifice to YHWH, “lest he fall on us (yifga’enu) with pestilence or with the sword” (5:3). At the lodging place it was “on the way” (ba-derekh) that YHWH “met him” (va-yifgeshehu); the two Hebrew verbs are close in sound and in sense, and in both verses a danger from YHWH stands on the way. Killing is spoken of again later in the unit: after Pharaoh has taken away the straw, the officers of the children of Israel charge Moses and Aaron with putting “a sword in their hand to kill us” (5:21). And near the end of the unit, when Moses is sent to Pharaoh again, he says of himself, “I am of uncircumcised lips” (aral sefatayim, 6:12), a word from the same root as the foreskin (orlah) that Zipporah cut off.

The unit does not explain what happened at the lodging place, but it keeps coming back to it. We will read the unit with these three verses in view, and come back to them at the end.

The Unit’s Boundaries

The unit opens by naming a place: “YHWH said to Moses in Midian, Go, return to Egypt” (4:19). Moses has been in Midian since he fled there from Pharaoh (2:15), and yet the place is named. The unit before this one ended with Jethro’s leave, “Go in peace” (4:18); this one begins with YHWH’s order, “Go, return.” A few verses later a second order is given to match the first: “YHWH said to Aaron, Go to meet Moses, to the wilderness” (4:27).

It closes with a list. “These are the heads of their fathers’ houses” (6:14): the sons of Reuben, the sons of Simeon, and then the sons of Levi, where the list stops running through the sons of Israel and follows one line down from father to son, from Levi to Kohath to Amram, until it reaches Aaron and Moses (6:20). The book began with the same list, “These are the names of the sons of Israel who came to Egypt” (1:1), Reuben, Simeon, Levi and the rest (1:2–4). The two lists enclose the first two units of the book and make them a pair. The first list names a people. The second ends with the two men who are to bring it out: “It is Aaron and Moses to whom YHWH said, Bring the children of Israel out of the land of Egypt” (6:26).

After the list comes one more verse, and it names a place again: “And it was, on the day YHWH spoke to Moses in the land of Egypt” (6:28). Read straight on, the verse sounds like the beginning of the next sentence. The Hebrew text sets a paragraph break after it, and what follows starts afresh: “YHWH spoke to Moses, saying, I am YHWH; speak to Pharaoh king of Egypt all that I speak to you” (6:29). The unit that opened “in Midian” closes “in the land of Egypt.”

The Unit’s Architecture

Like the unit before it, this unit is laid out in two columns and three rows. Each row is divided into smaller sections, marked a, b and c (and d in Row 3), and each section in Column A stands opposite a section in Column B. The table can be read in two directions: across a row, section opposite section, and down a column.

The three rows tell what becomes of YHWH’s message to Pharaoh. In Row 1 YHWH’s word is sent. Moses is ordered back to Egypt with a message for Pharaoh, Aaron is ordered out to meet him, and the word is spoken to the people and then to Pharaoh. Row 2 is Pharaoh’s answer. Neither Moses nor Aaron appears in it, and YHWH does not speak in it. The one who commands is Pharaoh, and his order goes out to the people in the form that YHWH’s word had: “Thus says Pharaoh” (5:10), after “Thus says YHWH” (4:22; 5:1). Row 3 is YHWH’s answer to what Pharaoh has done. In it the one who sends says who he is, “I am YHWH” (6:2), and the two who are sent are named: “It is Aaron and Moses” (6:26).

The columns divide between Moses alone and Moses with Aaron. In Column A YHWH speaks to Moses, and Aaron is not there. He is named in the column only once, when the officers come upon the two brothers as they leave Pharaoh (5:20), and then it is Moses alone who “returned to YHWH” (5:22). In Column B Aaron is beside him. YHWH speaks to Aaron (4:27), Aaron speaks the words to the people (4:30), and near the foot of the column YHWH speaks “to Moses and to Aaron” (6:13). Once in this column YHWH speaks to Moses alone (3Ba, 6:10–12), and Moses’ answer there, “I am of uncircumcised lips,” is followed at once by the charge to both brothers. At the end of the unit before this one Moses was told of Aaron, “he shall be to you for a mouth” (4:16). In this unit Aaron comes. In Row 2 neither brother appears. In Column A Pharaoh’s order goes down to the people through his taskmasters. In Column B the officers of the children of Israel, whom the taskmasters had set over the people, come and cry to Pharaoh (5:15).

The Rows Read Across

Row 1: YHWH’s Word Is Sent

Row 1 has three sections on each side. In Column A YHWH speaks to Moses, and everything in the column moves toward Egypt: “Go, return to Egypt” (4:19), “he returned to the land of Egypt” (4:20), “when you go to return to Egypt” (4:21). In Column B YHWH speaks to Aaron, and the column moves the other way. Aaron is sent “to the wilderness” (4:27), and what the brothers then ask of Pharaoh is that the people be let go there, “that they may hold a feast to me in the wilderness” (5:1).

The first pair is the two orders. “YHWH said to Moses in Midian, Go, return to Egypt” (1Aa, 4:19). “YHWH said to Aaron, Go to meet Moses, to the wilderness” (1Ba, 4:27). The brothers are sent toward each other with the same word, lekh, “go,” and this is the only time in Exodus that YHWH speaks to Aaron alone. The order to Moses comes with a death: “for all the men who sought your life are dead.” The order to Aaron comes with a meeting. Aaron “went and met him at the mountain of the deity, and kissed him,” and “Moses told Aaron all the words of YHWH with which he had sent him, and all the signs with which he had charged him” (4:27–28). In Column A Moses receives the word alone. In Column B, from its first section, he has someone to tell it to.

In the second pair Moses is told what to do before Pharaoh, and Aaron does what was to be done before the people. “See all the wonders (ha-mofetim) that I have put in your hand, and do them before Pharaoh” (1Ab, 4:21). “Aaron spoke all the words that YHWH had spoken to Moses, and did the signs (ha-otot) before the eyes of the people” (1Bb, 4:30). At the bush, what was put in Moses’ hand was called signs: “this staff you shall take in your hand, with which you shall do the signs” (4:17). Before Pharaoh it is given another name, and the name is new. “Wonder” is not found in the Torah before this verse.

We read the two names as two ways of taking what is in Moses’ hand. A sign stands for something else. At the bush the signs have a voice, “If they do not listen to the voice of the first sign, they will believe the voice of the latter sign” (4:8), and they are given “so that they may believe that YHWH … has appeared to you” (4:5). A sign tells of the one who is behind it, as a written word tells of what it names. So it is here: Aaron does the signs, “and the people believed, and they heard that YHWH had taken note of the children of Israel” (4:31). A wonder is a wonder in itself, and it need not point to anyone. The sentence that orders the wonders says in the same breath what will come of them: “and I will harden his heart, and he will not send the people” (4:21). When Pharaoh hears the name, he answers, “I do not know YHWH” (5:2). On this reading, what is in Moses’ hand is a sign to those who learn from it who has sent him, and a wonder to the one who does not know who that is. The next unit names the two together, “I will multiply my signs and my wonders in the land of Egypt” (7:3). In this row they are divided between the people and Pharaoh.

The third pair is the lodging place and the first audience with Pharaoh, and what the two sections share is that nothing has prepared us for either. In 1Ac YHWH has just told Moses what lies ahead, as far as the death of Pharaoh’s firstborn (4:21–23), and nothing in it says that YHWH will meet anyone on the way. Then “YHWH met him and sought to put him to death” (4:24). In 1Bc Moses and Aaron make the request that was given at the bush, in the words given there: “The deity of the Hebrews has met with us; let us go, please, a way of three days in the wilderness, and sacrifice to YHWH our deity” (5:3; 3:18). Then they add a clause that is in none of the words YHWH gave them: “lest he fall on us with pestilence or with the sword.” On one side YHWH does what no one had said he would do. On the other Moses and Aaron say what no one had told them to say, and what they say is that YHWH may come upon them with death. In both sections the danger from YHWH falls on Moses’ side and not on Pharaoh’s. Before Pharaoh the brothers say “lest he fall on us.” At the lodging place the death that had just been announced was the death of Pharaoh’s son, and the one whom YHWH met was of Moses’ own household.

In both sections someone answers the danger without being told to. Zipporah cuts off her son’s foreskin and speaks of blood, “and he let him go” (4:25–26). Moses and Aaron ask leave to go and sacrifice, “lest he fall on us.” In the one a cutting, and in the other a sacrifice, stands between YHWH and a death.

Three times in this row YHWH is the one who kills or may kill: “behold, I kill your son, your firstborn” (4:23); “sought to put him to death” (4:24); “lest he fall on us with pestilence or with the sword” (5:3). The unit never says that anyone is afraid. It lets the danger be heard three times, before anything has been done to Egypt.

Row 2: Pharaoh’s Answer

Near the end of Row 1 Pharaoh sends the brothers away: “Go to your burdens” (5:4). Row 2 is what he does next, “on that day” (5:6). Neither Moses nor Aaron appears anywhere in the row, and YHWH does not speak in it. The one who commands is Pharaoh, and his order reaches the people as YHWH’s word reached him, through others and in the same form: “The taskmasters of the people and its officers went out and said to the people, Thus says Pharaoh, I give you no straw” (2Ab, 5:10).

The message that Moses carries says what YHWH wants of Israel: “Send my son, that he may serve me (ve-ya’avdeni)” (4:23). Row 2 is filled with the same word, as Pharaoh uses it. “Let the work (ha-avodah) be heavy on the men” (2Aa, 5:9). When the officers of the children of Israel come before him, they call themselves “your servants (avadekha)” three times in two verses (2Bb, 5:15–16), and his last word to them is “Go, serve (lekhu ivdu)” (5:18). YHWH has asked that Israel serve him. Pharaoh’s answer is that they are his servants, and in this row they say so themselves.

Their cry, too, is turned toward Pharaoh. The people have been crying, in Pharaoh’s own words, “Let us go and sacrifice to our deity” (5:8). When the officers are beaten, they “came and cried to Pharaoh” (5:15).

The two columns of the row end on the same words. In Column A the taskmasters press the people: “Finish your work, each day’s share in its day, as when there was straw” (5:13). In Column B the officers of the children of Israel “saw themselves in trouble” under the same demand: “You shall not lessen your bricks, each day’s share in its day” (5:19). The demand that began in Column A as Pharaoh’s order falls in Column B on the officers, who are Israelites.

Row 2 stands between “that he may serve me” in Row 1 and “I will deliver you from their service” in Row 3 (6:6). It shows the service that is to pass from Pharaoh to YHWH, at its hardest.

Row 3: YHWH’s Answer

Row 3 has four sections on each side, and both columns follow one order: a complaint, an answer, a statement of who is speaking or who is sent, and the outcome.

The complaints come first. In 3Aa the officers come upon Moses and Aaron as they leave Pharaoh, and they call on YHWH against them: “May YHWH look on you and judge, for you have made us stink in the eyes of Pharaoh and in the eyes of his servants, to put a sword in their hand to kill us” (5:21). In 3Ba the complaint is Moses’ own, and it is about himself: “The children of Israel have not listened to me; how then will Pharaoh hear me, and I am of uncircumcised lips” (6:12). In Column A the two brothers are accused of having failed. In Column B Moses says it of himself.

Each complaint is answered. In Column A Moses has gone back to YHWH and asked, “Why did you send me (shelahtani)?” (5:22), and the answer takes up his verb: “Now you shall see what I will do to Pharaoh: for with a strong hand he will send them (yeshallehem), and with a strong hand he will drive them out of his land” (3Ab, 6:1). In Column B the answer to “I am of uncircumcised lips” is that YHWH now speaks to both brothers: “YHWH spoke to Moses and to Aaron, and gave them a charge to the children of Israel and to Pharaoh king of Egypt, to bring the children of Israel out of the land of Egypt” (3Bb, 6:13). The going out is spoken of twice, and the one who acts is different each time. In Column A Pharaoh will send the people and drive them out. In Column B Moses and Aaron are to bring them out.

In the third pair each column says who someone is. In 3Ac it is the one who sends. “Elohim spoke to Moses and said to him, I am YHWH” (6:2), and the speech says “I am YHWH” three times more before it ends (6:6, 7, 8). It names the fathers, Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, near its beginning and at its end (6:3, 8). Opposite it, in 3Bc, the story stops for a list: “And these are the names of the sons of Levi” (6:16). The list reaches Aaron and Moses through three fathers, Levi, Kohath and Amram, and it gives the years of each one’s life. In each column someone is made known through fathers: in Column A the one who sends, through the fathers to whom he appeared, and in Column B the two who are sent, through their own.

The speech also says what the fathers did not have. “I appeared (va-era) to Abraham, to Isaac and to Jacob as El Shaddai, and by my name YHWH I was not known (lo noda’ti) to them” (6:3). The two verbs are different. To appear is to be seen, and to be known is something more. The fathers saw, and they did not know. Column A makes the same step for the children of Israel. The answer to Moses begins, “Now you shall see (tir’eh)” (6:1), and the speech that follows promises, “you shall know (vi-yda’tem) that I am YHWH your deity, who brings you out from under the burdens of Egypt” (6:7). It is the difference between the wonder and the sign in Row 1. A wonder is seen. A sign, like a name, tells of the one behind it.

The last pair gives the outcome. In 3Ad Moses does as he was told and speaks to the people himself: “Moses spoke so to the children of Israel, and they did not listen to Moses, from shortness of spirit and from hard labour” (6:9). In 3Bd, after the list, the unit names its speakers: “It is Aaron and Moses to whom YHWH said, Bring the children of Israel out of the land of Egypt … They are the ones who speak to Pharaoh king of Egypt … it is Moses and Aaron” (6:26–27).

The two verses give the brothers in two orders, and we read them as two points of view. “Aaron and Moses” is the order in the verse that tells what YHWH said to them. Aaron is the elder (7:7) and was named first at their birth (6:20), and in this unit it is YHWH who speaks of the firstborn: “Israel is my son, my firstborn” (4:22). “Moses and Aaron” is the order in the verse that speaks of Pharaoh, and the next unit says how the two are to stand before him: “See, I have made you a deity to Pharaoh, and Aaron your brother shall be your prophet” (7:1). Before YHWH Aaron is first, as the firstborn. Before Pharaoh Moses is first, and Aaron speaks for him.

There is a second way to read Row 3. So far we have set each section of Column A beside the section that stands opposite it in Column B. But the row has eight sections, and the Torah tells them one after another: the four of Column A, and then the four of Column B. Read in that order, the first section and the eighth match each other, and so do the fourth and the fifth, the two in the middle.

The fourth section is the last one of Column A. Moses has brought YHWH’s promise to the people, “and they did not listen (ve-lo shame’u) to Moses” (3Ad, 6:9). The fifth section is the first one of Column B, and in it Moses says the same thing to YHWH in the same words: “The children of Israel have not listened (lo shame’u) to me” (3Ba, 6:12). What Column A tells as the outcome, Column B begins with as Moses’ complaint.

The first section and the eighth are both about Moses and Aaron and Pharaoh. In the first, the officers of the children of Israel come out from Pharaoh, where they have pleaded for the people and been refused, and they find Moses and Aaron standing and waiting for them (3Aa, 5:20). They blame the two brothers for what Pharaoh has done. The eighth section says who these two brothers are: “They are the ones who speak to Pharaoh king of Egypt, to bring the children of Israel out of Egypt; it is Moses and Aaron” (3Bd, 6:27). The officers have spoken to Pharaoh and failed, and the row ends by naming the two who are to speak to him. One Hebrew root joins the two sections: the officers “came out” (be-tsetam) from Pharaoh, and Moses and Aaron are to “bring out” (le-hotsi) the children of Israel. Read in this way, Row 3 begins and ends with Moses and Aaron and Pharaoh, and at its middle are the people, who are not listening.

Read across, Rows 1 and 3 set what happens when Moses is alone beside what happens when Aaron is with him. Row 2, where neither brother appears, does not fit that description, and to see what its two sides share with the others we have to read the columns.

The Columns Read Down

Until now we have read the table across, setting each section of Column A beside the section opposite it. We now read each column from top to bottom, through all three rows, and ask what the three rows of one column have in common.

The table heads Column A “Moses alone” and Column B “Moses with Aaron.” That is true of nearly all of Rows 1 and 3. It cannot be the whole difference between the columns, because in Row 2 neither Moses nor Aaron appears in either column.

The last verses of the unit before this one tell us what to look for. There Moses was told two things, one about Aaron and one about himself. About Aaron: “He shall speak for you to the people, and he shall be to you for a mouth, and you shall be to him for a deity” (4:16). About himself: “And this staff you shall take in your hand, with which you shall do the signs” (4:17). Aaron is to be a mouth, and Moses is to hold the staff in his hand. One brother speaks, and the other acts. The two columns of this unit divide in the same way. Column A is the column of the hand, and of what is done. Column B is the column of the mouth, and of what is said.

Column A: The Hand, and What Is Done

The Hebrew word for “hand” (yad) is used six times in this unit, and all six are in Column A. In Row 1, Moses sets out for Egypt with “the staff of the deity in his hand” (1Aa, 4:20), and YHWH speaks of “all the wonders that I have put in your hand” (1Ab, 4:21). In Row 3, the officers accuse Moses and Aaron of putting “a sword in their hand to kill us” (3Aa, 5:21). YHWH tells Moses that Pharaoh will send the people out “with a strong hand,” and says it twice (3Ab, 6:1). And in the speech that follows he speaks of the land “that I lifted my hand to give to Abraham, to Isaac and to Jacob” (3Ac, 6:8). In Column B the word is not used at all.

What is done in Column A is done by force. Each of the three sections of Row 1 has a death in it. In the first, Moses can go back because “all the men who sought your life are dead” (1Aa, 4:19). In the second, YHWH gives him a threat to carry to Pharaoh: “behold, I kill your son, your firstborn” (1Ab, 4:23). In the third, “YHWH met him and sought to put him to death” (1Ac, 4:24). These three words of death run down the column, and there is a fourth in Row 3, where the officers say that Moses and Aaron have put a sword in the hand of Pharaoh and his servants “to kill us” (3Aa, 5:21).

In Row 2 of this column no one is killed. Here the force is Pharaoh’s, and he uses it against the people by making them work harder. “Let the work be heavy on the men,” he orders (2Aa, 5:9), and his taskmasters carry the order out: “The taskmasters pressed, saying, Finish your work, each day’s share in its day, as when there was straw” (2Ab, 5:13).

Row 3 begins in the same way. Its first section is the officers’ charge about the sword. In its second section Moses goes back to YHWH and asks, “Why have you done evil to this people?” (3Ab, 5:22), and YHWH answers by telling him what he will now do to Pharaoh, “with a strong hand” (6:1). So from the first verse of the column to this point, every section tells of a death, of a threat, or of a burden laid on someone.

The column changes in the third section of Row 3. “Elohim spoke to Moses and said to him, I am YHWH” (3Ac, 6:2), and for the first time in Column A YHWH sends word to the people, and it is a promise: “I will bring you out from under the burdens of Egypt, and I will deliver you from their service, and I will redeem you with an outstretched arm and with great judgments” (6:6). Force is still spoken of here, in the outstretched arm and the great judgments. Some of the force in the column was already aimed at Pharaoh: the threat to his firstborn (1Ab, 4:23), and the strong hand of the section before this one (3Ab, 6:1). But until now the only word that has come to the people in this column is Pharaoh’s (2Ab, 5:10). Here YHWH’s word comes to them, and they are told that the force is for them. The word “judgments” shows the change. Two sections earlier the officers had called on YHWH to judge Moses and Aaron: “May YHWH look on you and judge (ve-yishpot)” (3Aa, 5:21). In the promise, the judgments (shefatim, from the same Hebrew root) are the means by which YHWH will redeem the people.

Who is it that acts in Column A? In each row it is a ruler, using his own power. In Rows 1 and 3 it is YHWH. He gives Moses his orders, he threatens Pharaoh’s son, he himself comes to the lodging place, and he says what he will do to Pharaoh. In Row 2 it is Pharaoh. “Pharaoh commanded on that day the taskmasters over the people and its officers” (2Aa, 5:6), and the next section follows his command until it reaches the people in his own words: “Thus says Pharaoh, I give you no straw” (2Ab, 5:10). In Column A, then, we see in every row the one from whom a command comes, and what his power does. We may call it the column of the source of authority.

The column ends with Pharaoh’s power still in place. When Pharaoh made the work heavier, he said what he wanted from it: “let them do it, and let them not regard words of falsehood” (2Aa, 5:9). He wanted the people so occupied with their work that they would pay no attention to talk of leaving it. The last section of the column shows that he got what he wanted. Moses brings the people YHWH’s promise, “and they did not listen to Moses, from shortness of spirit and from hard labour” (3Ad, 6:9). The Hebrew word for “labour” here, avodah, is the word Pharaoh used when he said, “Let the work (ha-avodah) be heavy.”

The last section also shows why Moses, who speaks in it, stands in this column. Moses is to lead the people and bring them out, and for that the people must accept him. The section says that they do not: “Moses spoke so to the children of Israel, and they did not listen to Moses” (3Ad, 6:9). The verse names him twice. It is Moses to whom they do not listen. The officers had already refused him at the beginning of the row, when they called on YHWH to judge him and Aaron (3Aa, 5:21). Column A shows those who have authority, and in Row 3 it shows that Moses does not yet have it with his own people.

Column B: The Mouth, and What Is Said

Column B ends with a sentence that describes the whole column: “They are the ones who speak to Pharaoh” (3Bd, 6:27).

The column is about speaking from its first section. In Row 1 each of its three sections tells of words being passed on. In the first, YHWH speaks to Aaron, and Moses tells Aaron “all the words of YHWH” (1Ba, 4:27–28). In the second, Aaron speaks those words to the people (1Bb, 4:30). In the third, the two brothers speak to Pharaoh: “Thus says YHWH” (1Bc, 5:1). These three sections stand opposite the three sections of Column A that each have a death in them. Where Column A has a death, Column B has a speech.

Speaking to Pharaoh is found only in this column. In Column A, YHWH tells Moses what he is to say to Pharaoh (1Ab, 4:22), but Moses does not say it there. The words are said to Pharaoh in Column B: “Moses and Aaron came and said to Pharaoh, Thus says YHWH, the deity of Israel, Send my people” (1Bc, 5:1). In Row 2 it is again in Column B that someone stands before Pharaoh and speaks: “The officers of the children of Israel came and cried to Pharaoh” (2Bb, 5:15). And in Row 3 it is in Column B that Moses is ordered, “Go in, speak to Pharaoh king of Egypt” (3Ba, 6:11).

Everyone who speaks in Column B is speaking for someone else. In Row 1 three phrases show the words of YHWH passing from one speaker to the next. “Moses told Aaron all the words of YHWH with which he had sent him” (4:28). “Aaron spoke all the words that YHWH had spoken to Moses” (4:30). “They said to Pharaoh, Thus says YHWH” (5:1). The words come from YHWH to Moses, from Moses to Aaron, and from the two brothers to Pharaoh. When Moses later looks back on that first audience, he describes it in the same way: “since I came to Pharaoh to speak in your name” (5:23).

In Row 2 the ones who speak for others are the officers. Two groups of men stand between Pharaoh and the people in this row. The first are Pharaoh’s taskmasters, who drive the people at their work. The second are “the officers of the children of Israel, whom Pharaoh’s taskmasters had set over them” (2Ba, 5:14): these are Israelites, appointed by the taskmasters over their own people. The two columns of Row 2 divide the two groups between them. In Column A the taskmasters come first, and the officers are named only together with them, as “the taskmasters of the people and its officers” (5:6, 10). Together they carry Pharaoh’s order down to the people. In Column B the officers stand by themselves. Three times they are called “the officers of the children of Israel” (5:14, 15, 19). Here they are not carrying Pharaoh’s order. They are beaten because the bricks fall short, and they go to Pharaoh to speak for themselves and for their people: “Why do you do so to your servants? Straw is not given to your servants, and they say to us, Make bricks; and behold, your servants are beaten” (2Bb, 5:15–16). The officers cannot change what is being done. All that they can do is speak. Like Moses and Aaron in Row 1, they stand before Pharaoh and speak to him on behalf of Israel.

In Row 3 the mouth itself is named. Moses doubts that he is able to speak: “How then will Pharaoh hear me, and I am of uncircumcised lips” (3Ba, 6:12). This is the only place in the unit where a mouth or lips are mentioned, as “hand” is mentioned only in Column A. YHWH answers by giving the task to both brothers together (3Bb, 6:13). Then comes the list of names, which presents the brothers among “the heads of their fathers’ houses” (3Bc, 6:14), and then they are named as the speakers: “It is Aaron and Moses … They are the ones who speak to Pharaoh” (3Bd, 6:26–27).

Row 3 also describes the two brothers in the words that Column A uses of YHWH. In Column A, YHWH says, “I will bring you out (ve-hotseti)” (3Ac, 6:6). In Column B the same verb is given to Moses and Aaron. They are charged “to bring out (le-hotsi)” the children of Israel (3Bb, 6:13), and they are the ones to whom YHWH said, “Bring out (hotsi’u) the children of Israel” (3Bd, 6:26). In Column A, YHWH says who he is: “I am YHWH.” In Column B the Torah says who they are: “It is Aaron and Moses.”

The unit before this one did the same thing with Moses and the people. It described Moses in words that it had first used of the people: the people came to Egypt “each man and his house” (1:1), and the story of Moses begins with “a man of the house of Levi” (2:1). Then it sent him to speak for them. Moses represents the people in two senses: he is like them, and he speaks for them. In this unit Moses and Aaron represent YHWH in the same two senses. They are described in his words, and they speak in his name. Column B is the column of those who represent authority: they speak for the one who has it.

Moses in Both Columns

Moses has a part in both columns, and his part is different in each. In Column A he is the one who is sent. YHWH gives him his orders, and when the mission seems to have failed he asks, “Why did you send me?” (3Ab, 5:22). In Column B he passes on what he was given: “Moses told Aaron all the words of YHWH with which he had sent him” (1Ba, 4:28), and it is Aaron who then speaks them (4:30). Toward YHWH, Moses is the one who is told what to say. Toward Aaron, he is the one who tells. This is what he was told about Aaron at the bush: “you shall be to him for a deity” (4:16).

Row 2 has the same order of command on Pharaoh’s side. Pharaoh gives his order to the taskmasters, and the taskmasters stand over the officers of the children of Israel. So the unit sets two chains of command side by side. In one, YHWH commands Moses, and Moses has Aaron to speak for him. In the other, Pharaoh commands his taskmasters, and under them are the officers.

In both chains those below turn to the one at the top and ask him the same question. The officers cry to Pharaoh, “Why (lammah) do you do so to your servants?” (2Bb, 5:15). Moses says to YHWH, “Why (lammah) have you done evil to this people?” (3Ab, 5:22). The two answers show the difference between the two rulers. Pharaoh sends the officers back to their work: “Go, serve” (5:18). YHWH tells Moses that he himself will now act: “Now you shall see what I will do to Pharaoh” (6:1).

Not every verse falls on the side where we would expect it. Some things are done in Column B: Aaron “did the signs before the eyes of the people” (4:30), and the officers are beaten (5:14). But the main lines are clear. Every “hand” is in Column A, and so is the staff. The one mention of lips, and every speech to Pharaoh, are in Column B.

One Hebrew root connects the two columns, and it connects them through this same difference. The Hebrew words for “foreskin” and “uncircumcised” share a root, and the unit has it twice. The first time is in Column A, in Row 1, at the lodging place: Zipporah “cut off the foreskin (orlat) of her son” (1Ac, 4:25). The second time is in Column B, in Row 3, where Moses says, “I am of uncircumcised (aral) lips” (3Ba, 6:12). In Column A, the column of what is done, it is said of the body, and the trouble is ended by an act: Zipporah takes a flint and cuts. In Column B, the column of what is said, it is said of speech, and the trouble is met by giving Moses his brother to speak with him.

The Unit in the Book

Exodus is built of nineteen units. Three of them stand alone: the sea, the covenant and the tablets. The other sixteen fall into four quarters of four units each, and the first quarter is Egypt, Units 1 to 4. Laid out as a map, the four quarters stand in a square, with the sea, the covenant and the tablets down the middle, as on the small map at the head of this commentary (Figure 1), and here with the names of the units:

Units 1 and 2: A Pair

The unit before this one leaves Moses alone with a task he did not want. At the bush he raised one objection after another, and the last was hardly an objection at all: “Send, please, by the hand of whom you will send” (4:13). The anger of YHWH burned against him, and the answer was a brother: “Is there not Aaron your brother the Levite? I know that he can surely speak; and behold, he is coming out to meet you” (4:14). Aaron “shall be to you for a mouth, and you shall be to him for a deity” (4:16). The first unit of the pair is the choosing of Moses, and it ends with the promise that he will not go alone. The second unit keeps the promise.

Aaron does not hesitate as Moses did. YHWH speaks to him once, “Go to meet Moses, to the wilderness,” and he goes, meets him at the mountain of the deity, and kisses him (4:27). He never speaks a word of his own in the unit. What he says is “all the words that YHWH had spoken to Moses” (4:30), and that is the whole of his part: he is the one through whom Moses’ word is heard. Whenever Moses goes to the people or to Pharaoh, Aaron is with him, and the king of Egypt rebukes the two of them in one breath: “Why, Moses and Aaron, do you take the people from their work?” (5:4). The unit closes by naming them together as the ones YHWH charged: “They are the ones who speak to Pharaoh king of Egypt … it is Moses and Aaron” (6:27). The next unit says what Aaron has become: “See, I have made you a deity to Pharaoh, and Aaron your brother shall be your prophet” (7:1). As the columns showed, Moses stood for the people in the unit before this one; in this one, with Aaron beside him, he stands for YHWH before Pharaoh.

The pair also shows YHWH becoming known, first to one man and then to more, and then the widening stops. In the first unit YHWH is revealed to one man, and tells him what is to come: the elders “will listen to your voice,” and the king of Egypt “will not let you go” (3:18–19). In the second the word passes from YHWH to Moses, from Moses to Aaron, and from Aaron to the people, and at first it is believed. Aaron does the signs “before the eyes of the people,” and “the people believed” (4:30–31). For a moment the whole people believes what Moses was told at the bush: that YHWH has taken note of them. Then the king answers as YHWH said he would, “I do not know YHWH” (5:2), and makes the work heavier, and under the labour the people stop listening. When Moses brings them YHWH’s promise, “they did not listen to Moses, from shortness of spirit and from hard labour” (6:9). The verse gives its own reason, and the reason is the labour, not doubt. Words and signs are not enough for a people under that burden. What they need is to be brought out, and that is where the next unit begins: “I will put my hand on Egypt, and bring out my hosts, my people the children of Israel” (7:4). The Torah does not say again that the people believed until they have crossed the sea (14:31).

One thing is reversed between the two units. In the first unit it is Pharaoh who seeks a death. He orders the Hebrew sons killed (1:16), and when Moses has struck the Egyptian, “Pharaoh sought to kill (va-yevakkesh laharog) Moses” (2:15). The second unit opens by telling Moses that those men are dead (4:19), and then the same verbs are said of YHWH: “I kill (horeg) your son, your firstborn” (4:23), and at the lodging place “he sought to put him to death (va-yevakkesh hamito)” (4:24). Moses fled Egypt from the one who sought his death. Now the one who seeks a death is the one who sent him back.

The Lodging Place and the Night of the Going Out

Directly beneath this unit on the map is Unit 4, the night of the going out. In three verses on the way to Egypt, three things happen in Moses’ own household: a son is circumcised, there is blood, and a death passes by (1Ac, 4:24–26). On the night of the going out the same three things happen at every door in Israel. Each house takes a lamb and puts its blood on the doorposts, “and there shall be no plague among you” (12:13), and the law of the meal is that “no uncircumcised man shall eat of it” (12:48). The death that passes by on that night is the one announced in this unit, in the message that Moses carries: “Israel is my son, my firstborn … behold, I kill your son, your firstborn” (1Ab, 4:22–23). At midnight “YHWH struck every firstborn in the land of Egypt” (12:29), and the firstborn of Israel are made his: “Sanctify to me every firstborn … it is mine” (13:2). What Zipporah did for one household, in haste and with a flint, becomes the law of every house.

This unit leaves two refusals standing. Pharaoh will not send the people, and his last word to their officers is “Go, serve (lekhu ivdu)” (2Bb, 5:18), which here means that they are to go back to work. The people, who had heard Aaron and “bowed and worshipped” (1Bb, 4:31), no longer listen. On the night of the going out both refusals are undone. Moses calls the elders and tells them of the Passover, “and the people bowed and worshipped” (12:27). Exodus says this of the people in these two places only. The first time, what came next was the straw. The second time, “the children of Israel went and did as YHWH had commanded Moses and Aaron” (12:28). And Pharaoh, in the middle of the night, calls for Moses and Aaron and uses his own words again: “Rise, go out from among my people … and go, serve YHWH (lekhu ivdu et YHWH), as you have said” (12:31).

Unit 2 stands at the head of the second long column of the map, and the units beneath Unit 4 keep to what begins at the lodging place: each of the people becomes YHWH’s, and whoever comes before him is in danger of death. In the plan of the tabernacle Aaron goes into the holy place with the names of the sons of Israel on his shoulders (28:12) and with bells on his robe, “so that he does not die” (28:35). Every man who is counted gives half a shekel, “a ransom for his life … so that there be no plague among them” (30:12). The column runs on into Leviticus, where Aaron is told how he may come behind the veil, “so that he does not die” (Lev 16:2).

The Mountain

Aaron meets Moses “at the mountain of the deity” (1Ba, 4:27). It is the mountain of the bush, where Moses was told, “you shall serve the deity on this mountain” (3:12). Every unit in the top row of the map comes back to that mountain, and at the other end of the row, in Unit 7, the people camp there. Moses’ father-in-law brings his wife and his sons, who set out with him in this unit (4:20), back to him “at the mountain of the deity,” and Moses goes out to meet him “and kissed him” (18:5, 7), as Aaron had met Moses there and kissed him.

What Moses and Aaron ask of Pharaoh in this unit is leave for the people to go into the wilderness, “that they may hold a feast to me in the wilderness” (1Bc, 5:1), and to sacrifice to YHWH, “lest he fall on us with pestilence or with the sword” (5:3). The last clause was their own, and what happens at the mountain confirms it. When YHWH comes down on Sinai he warns twice that the people, and even the priests, must keep back, “lest YHWH break out against them” (19:22, 24). The danger that the brothers named before Pharaoh is the danger that YHWH himself names at the mountain.

At the mountain the promise of this unit is spoken as something done. Here “Elohim spoke to Moses and said to him, I am YHWH,” and promised, “you shall know that I am YHWH your deity, who brings you out from under the burdens of Egypt” (3Ac, 6:2, 7). Exodus says “Elohim spoke” in one other place, at Sinai, to the whole people: “Elohim spoke all these words, saying: I am YHWH your deity, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slaves” (20:1–2). Pharaoh had said, “I do not know YHWH” (5:2). Before the people reach Sinai, a priest of Midian says, “Now I know that YHWH is greater than all the deities” (18:11).

The people who “did not listen to Moses” (3Ad, 6:9) now ask to listen to him. “You speak with us, and we will hear,” they say, “but let not Elohim speak with us, lest we die” (20:15 [English 20:19]). This is what began in this unit with Aaron, now asked for by the whole people: one hears the words and passes them on. Moses was told that he would be “for a deity” to Aaron (4:16), and Aaron spoke his words to the people. At the mountain the people themselves ask that Moses stand between: “The people stood far off, and Moses drew near to the thick darkness where the deity was” (20:17 [English 20:21]). The unit of the tablets tells the same thing of a face that cannot be looked at. Moses asks to see YHWH’s glory and is told, “You cannot see my face” (33:20). When he comes down from the mountain his own face shines, the people “were afraid to come near him” (34:30), and he covers it with a veil (34:33). Moses stands toward the deity as the people stand toward Moses.

From Pharaoh’s Service to YHWH’s

The message for Pharaoh says in one word what YHWH wants of Israel: “Send my son, that he may serve me” (1Ab, 4:23). As we saw in Row 2, service and labour are one word in Hebrew (avodah), and in this unit it is always Pharaoh’s. “Let the work (ha-avodah) be heavy on the men” (2Aa, 5:9). The people do not listen to Moses “from hard labour (avodah)” (3Ad, 6:9). YHWH’s promise is “I will deliver you from their service (me-avodatam)” (3Ac, 6:6). Later in the book the same word is used of serving YHWH. On the night of the going out the Passover is called by it: “you shall keep this service” (12:25). In the plan of the tabernacle each man’s half shekel goes “for the service of the tent of meeting” (30:16). When the building begins, no one stands over the people, and the materials are brought by “every man whose heart lifted him” (35:21). And when the tabernacle has been made, the Torah says, “All the service (avodat) of the tabernacle of the tent of meeting was finished (va-tekhel)” (39:32). The verb is the one the taskmasters used when they pressed the people: “Finish (kallu) your work, each day’s share in its day” (2Ab, 5:13). In Egypt the people are driven to finish Pharaoh’s work. At the end of the book they finish YHWH’s.

The speech that promises deliverance from Pharaoh’s service also holds the sentence that joins the two halves of the book. “I will take you to me for a people, and I will be your deity, and you shall know that I am YHWH your deity, who brings you out from under the burdens of Egypt” (3Ac, 6:7). In the plan of the tabernacle the sentence returns with its purpose added: “I will dwell in the midst of the children of Israel, and I will be their deity. And they shall know that I am YHWH their deity, who brought them out of the land of Egypt to dwell in their midst” (29:45–46). In this unit the people cannot hear the sentence. By the end of the book they have made the tabernacle in which he dwells in their midst.

Reading the Unit

We began with the most difficult verse of the unit: “On the way, at the lodging place, YHWH met him and sought to put him to death” (4:24). YHWH had just sent Moses on that way. The verse gives no reason, and it does not say whose death was sought. We said that we would not explain these verses away. What the reading has shown is where they stand in the unit.

Nearly everything in this unit is speech. Aaron is added to Moses so that the two may speak for YHWH before Pharaoh. In Row 1 YHWH’s message is sent to Pharaoh, in Row 2 Pharaoh answers it, and in Row 3 YHWH answers Pharaoh. Pharaoh’s answer is a question, “Who is YHWH, that I should hear his voice, to send Israel?” (1Bc, 5:2), and an order: “Go to your burdens” (5:4). Row 3 answers both. “I am YHWH,” the speech to Moses says, four times, and “I will bring you out from under the burdens of Egypt” (3Ac, 6:6). The unit has the word “burdens” in these two sections only. But the answer is a promise, and the people to whom Moses brings it do not listen (6:9). Moses has already said to YHWH how things stand: “you have not delivered your people at all” (3Ab, 5:23). What YHWH will do is still to come: “Now you shall see what I will do to Pharaoh” (6:1).

Everywhere else in the unit YHWH speaks: he says, he gives a charge, he promises. Once he does something. At the lodging place he “met him and sought to put him to death,” and then “let him go” (1Ac, 4:24, 26). The one thing that YHWH does in this unit, he does on the way to Egypt, in the household of the man he has sent. When the unit ends, Pharaoh does not know YHWH and the people are no longer listening, and the only place to which YHWH himself has come is that lodging place. So when Moses and Aaron add to the words they were given, “lest he fall on us with pestilence or with the sword” (5:3), they say “us,” and the danger that they name to the king has already come to Moses’ own house. The officers of the children of Israel, who were not at the lodging place, speak of another sword, the one in Pharaoh’s hand (5:21).

Exodus has the words “on the way” (ba-derekh) three times more. At the mountain Moses tells his father-in-law of “all the hardship that had found them on the way, and YHWH delivered them” (18:8); the deliverance that he said had not come has come. When the covenant is made, YHWH sends a messenger before the people “to guard you on the way,” and warns them about him: “he will not pardon your transgression, for my name is in his midst” (23:20–21). After the golden calf YHWH says why a messenger must go and not he: “I will not go up in your midst, for you are a stiff-necked people, lest I consume you on the way” (33:3). Here YHWH himself says what his presence on the way can do to his own people. At the bush Moses was promised, “I will be with you” (3:12). We read the lodging place as the first time the book shows that YHWH’s being with someone on the way can be a danger to the very ones he is with.

That is not an explanation. After the calf a reason is given, and at the lodging place there is none. What the unit does is set two things in one column of its table. In Row 3 YHWH says who he is: “I am YHWH … and I will bring you out” (3Ac, 6:6). In Row 1 nothing is said. Someone is met on the way, a death is sought, and it is turned aside with blood (1Ac). On the night of the going out the two come together: YHWH passes through Egypt, and Israel is brought out (12:12, 12:51). In this unit they stand apart, and the Torah does not join them for us. The unit that opened “in Midian” closes “in the land of Egypt” (6:28), with the two brothers named and nothing yet done to Pharaoh, and with the lodging place behind them, unexplained.

For Further Reflection

What follows is offered as reasoned speculation. It does not rest on the table of the unit, as the rest of this commentary does. It reaches back into Genesis and forward to the night of the going out. Its first part asks what the three verses of the lodging place might be about, and its second takes up the two verbs of 6:3.

The House on the Way

The words just before the lodging place divide two houses: “Israel is my son, my firstborn … behold, I kill your son, your firstborn” (4:22–23). From here on there is YHWH’s firstborn, and there is Pharaoh’s. The household that is on the way could be counted with either. Moses “became her son,” the son of Pharaoh’s daughter (2:10). The daughters of the priest of Midian took him for “an Egyptian man” (2:19), and one of them is his wife. His son was born in Midian and named for it, “I have been a stranger (ger) in a foreign (nokhriyah) land” (2:22), and the son has not been circumcised. We may ask whether the death just announced for the house of Pharaoh comes first to the one house that stands on both sides, and whether what turns it aside is the act that makes a house Israel’s.

The law of the Passover speaks in the two words of the son’s name. “No son of a foreigner (ben nekhar) shall eat of it” (12:43). But “when a stranger (ger) sojourns with you and would make the Passover to YHWH, let every male of his be circumcised, and then he shall come near to make it, and he shall be as a native of the land” (12:48). At the lodging place it is the Midianite wife who does this, and the son is called hers: Zipporah “cut off her son’s foreskin” (4:25). Read in this way, the verses do not need to say whether “him” is the father or the son. Both are in question, and the house is one.

Genesis tells of another man on his way back to his brother, and it uses the same word. The verb “meet” (pagash) is found in the Torah in two stories only: Jacob’s return to Esau (Gen 32:18 [English 32:17]; 33:8) and Moses’ return to Egypt (4:24, 27). Jacob had fled from a brother who meant to kill him (Gen 27:41–43), and YHWH told him, “Return to the land of your fathers … and I will be with you” (Gen 31:3), the promise that Moses is given at the bush (3:12). He comes back with his wives and his children, and “he lodged there that night” (Gen 32:14 [English 32:13]), in the verb from which “lodging place” is made. In the night a man wrestles with him and touches (va-yigga) the hollow of his thigh; Jacob will not let him go, and he is given the name Israel (Gen 32:25–29 [English 32:24–28]). In the morning Esau runs to meet him “and kissed him” (Gen 33:4). Moses, too, fled from one who sought to kill him (2:15), and is told, “Go, return” (4:19). He sets out with his wife and his sons. At the lodging place the foreskin is made to touch (va-tagga) “his feet,” and “he let him go” (4:25–26). The name Israel has just been spoken, “Israel is my son, my firstborn,” and three verses after the lodging place Aaron meets his brother “and kissed him” (4:27). Jacob comes away from his night with the name Israel. If the two returns are told alike, the house of Moses may come away from the lodging place as a house of Israel.

Genesis also tells what YHWH may do to the firstborn of a house that has gone away from its brothers. Judah “went down from his brothers” and took the daughter of a Canaanite (Gen 38:1–2). She bore him sons, and “Er, Judah’s firstborn, was evil in the eyes of YHWH, and YHWH put him to death” (va-yemitehu, Gen 38:7), and then the second son “also” (38:10). The verb is the verb of the lodging place, “sought to put him to death” (hamito), and these two are the only persons whom Genesis says YHWH put to death. There too a woman acts without being told, and the house of Judah goes on through Tamar (Gen 38:26–30). The differences are plain. In Genesis a reason is given, and the sons die. At the lodging place no reason is given, and the death is turned aside.

One more journey stands behind this one. When Jacob himself set out for Egypt, he was spoken to in the night: “Elohim said to Israel in visions of the night, Jacob, Jacob. And he said, Here I am. And he said, I am the El, the deity of your father; do not fear to go down to Egypt … I will go down with you to Egypt, and I will surely bring you up” (Gen 46:2–4). The bush takes up nearly every part of this: “Moses, Moses,” “Here I am,” “I am the deity of your father,” and “I have come down … to bring them up” (3:4, 6, 8). The list of names that follows Jacob’s night, “These are the names of the sons of Israel who came to Egypt” (Gen 46:8), is the list with which Exodus opens (1:1), and its first names are those of the list that closes this unit (6:14–16). What Moses is not given is “do not fear.” Jacob, going down to Egypt, is told at night that the deity of his father goes down with him. Moses, going back there, has the lodging place on his way.

Seeing and Knowing

A second question reaches back further still. The speech of Row 3 says of the fathers, “I appeared … and by my name YHWH I was not known to them” (6:3), and we read its two verbs as two different things: to be seen, and to be known. The story of the garden sets the two side by side and joins them. The trees of the garden are “desirable to the sight” (Gen 2:9). The serpent promises, “your eyes will be opened, and you will be as elohim, knowing good and evil” (Gen 3:5). The woman sees that the tree is “a delight to the eyes” and “desirable for making wise” (Gen 3:6), and when the two have eaten, “the eyes of both of them were opened, and they knew” (Gen 3:7). In that story, and nowhere else in Genesis, the two names are joined: it is “YHWH Elohim” who plants the garden and who sends the man out of it (Gen 2:8; 3:23). After the garden the names are told one at a time, and in this unit seeing and knowing are apart as well. The fathers saw and did not know. Pharaoh does not know. Moses is told, “Now you shall see” (6:1), and Israel is promised, “you shall know” (6:7). Whether the joining of the two names and the joining of the two verbs belong together is a question for the reading of the Torah as a whole.

None of this is in the table of the unit, and a full reading would have to set the unit beside each of these stories as a whole, and not beside single verses. We offer it as a direction for further study.
