Part D: Architecture and Meaning in Exodus

The Book of Place

Introduction: From the Page to the Torah

Part A found the nineteen units of Exodus and the pairs they fall into. Part B built the page: two books, the story above and the dwelling below, each book two blocks and each block two pairs, with the sea, the covenant and the calf standing between the parts, and the covenant at the centre. Part C read the page both ways. Across it, the word given to one man at the left is taken up by all Israel at the right; down it, the sons of Israel who came to Egypt each with his house become one house, one dwelling; and the two directions cross in the covenant, where all the people answer with one voice. The architecture is in place. In the commentary on Genesis, this was the point at which I asked what the architecture means.

For Exodus the question has a different shape, because Exodus does not stand alone. It comes after Genesis, and the names that Genesis separated, YHWH and Elohim, come into it already separated. And it stands above Leviticus. On the map of the whole Torah the five books make a cross, and Exodus is the first book of the vertical arm, with Leviticus under it and Numbers under that. Part C ended with two questions, and both of them point outside the book. What becomes of the five columns of the page when they run on down into the two books below it, and of the three single units, standing in one column, beside the single units of the other books? And what does it mean that a book which begins with the names of the sons of Israel ends with all the house of Israel watching the glory of YHWH fill the dwelling?

This part takes the questions in that order:

  • The cross of the five books, so that the reader can see where Exodus stands.
  • The columns run on. The page of Exodus is set on the pages of Leviticus and Numbers, and what runs down through the three is read by the positions that have proved to be marks in every book of the Torah, the corners and the middles.
  • The borders of Sinai, a frame that is not a column.
  • The three single units, read against the single units of the three horizontal books.
  • The rows of the three vertical books, followed from the top of Exodus to the end of Numbers, where one thing is found flowing down them.
  • The thread that runs through the whole book: the revelation of YHWH and the establishment of Israel are one matter, told twice.

The Cross of the Five Books

On the page “Understanding the Torah Weave Map” I have set out the five books as a cross. Genesis, Leviticus and Deuteronomy run across it, and Exodus, Leviticus and Numbers run down it. Leviticus, the book the two arms share, stands where they cross. The three books that run across are the three books of three rows, and together they are a line of time: from the creation, through the fathers, to the death of Moses on the mountain over the land. The three books that run down are the two “quadratic” books, Exodus and Numbers, with Leviticus between them, and they are about place. They hold the Sinai pericope, which begins in the third month of the first year (19:1) and ends when the camp strikes in the second month of the second year (Numbers 1:1; 10:11), and which is spread across all three of them: the second book of Exodus, the whole of Leviticus, and the first column of Numbers. Exodus tells how a place for meeting comes to be, from the mountain to the dwelling. Numbers is the camp, laid out round the dwelling and then on the move. Leviticus, at the crossing, is the dwelling itself. Its twenty-two units stand in three rings round chapter 19, like the court, the holy place and the holy of holies, and chapter 19, “you shall be holy, for I YHWH your deity am holy” (19:2), stands in the middle like the ark with the tablets in it.

The seam between Exodus and Leviticus is the clearest in the Torah. Exodus ends with the dwelling raised and the glory filling it, so that “Moses was not able to enter the tent of meeting” (40:35). Leviticus begins with the voice from inside: “he called to Moses, and YHWH spoke to him from the tent of meeting” (Leviticus 1:1). Numbers begins there too, “in the tent of meeting, on the first day of the second month” (Numbers 1:1), one month after the dwelling was raised “on the first day of the first month” (Exodus 40:2, 17). So when the page of Exodus is laid out as Part B laid it, with the dwelling below the story, the second book of Exodus sits directly on Leviticus, and the lower edge of the page is the upper edge of the next. That is the reason for laying the page out this way rather than another. Inside Exodus, the page could stand on its side and show the same relationships. Laid flat, with the dwelling at the foot, its columns can run on.

The Columns Run On

Leviticus has eight columns, read across the page, with its single unit, chapter 19, in the fifth. One of the eight, the three units of the impurities (chapters 13–15), stands to one side of the main line, and I set it aside here, as I do whenever Leviticus is compared with another book. That leaves seven columns with chapter 19 in the middle and three on either side. Numbers has five columns, for its page is the camp: the dwelling at the centre, three units down each side, three across the top and three across the bottom, the corners shared. Exodus has five.

The three books are matched from their centres outward. The covenant stands over chapter 19 of Leviticus, and chapter 19 over the centre of the camp. On either side, Exodus’s two inner columns fall on the two columns of Leviticus next to chapter 19 and on the two short columns of Numbers; its two outer columns fall on the next columns out. Leviticus’s outermost columns on each side, chapters 1–7 and 20–27, stand beyond Exodus and beyond Numbers. The cross is widest at its crossing.

Exodus first column (1, 3, 11, 13) second column (2, 4, 12, 14) middle column (5, 10, 15) fourth column (6, 8, 16, 18) last column (7, 9, 17, 19)
Leviticus chapters 8–12 (Units 4, 5, 6) chapters 16–18 (Units 10, 11, 12) chapter 19 (Unit 13) chapters 20–22:25 (Units 14, 15, 16) chapters 22:26–24 (Units 17, 18, 19)
Numbers chapters 1–10 (Units 1, 2, 3) chapters 11–14 (Units 4, 5) chapters 15–19 (Units 6, 7, 8) chapters 20–25 (Units 9, 10) chapters 26–36 (Units 11, 12, 13)

The festivals anchor the matching; the positions tested in the next section are what carry it. The nation’s calendar is given three times in the three books: in the second half of the Covenant Code, “three times in the year you shall keep a festival to me” (23:14), in Leviticus 23, and in the offerings of every day, Sabbath, new moon and festival in Numbers 28–29. On the page of Exodus the calendar is in Unit 9, at the lower right of the story. Straight below it in Leviticus is Unit 18, chapter 23. Straight below that in Numbers is Unit 12, chapters 28–30. The three calendars stand in one column. In Leviticus and Numbers each is the middle unit of its column of three; in Exodus, whose columns hold four, Unit 9 is one of the inner pair. The firstfruits, which Exodus names only in Unit 9 (23:16, 19), Leviticus names only in chapter 23 and Numbers only in chapter 28 (28:26), fall in the same column. If the books are matched any other way, the calendars part company. Notice where the line falls: not down the centre, but two columns to the right of it in every book, in Exodus’s last column, in Leviticus’s seventh, in Numbers’ fifth. That is what makes it an anchor. The centres fix where the centres go; the calendar, standing two places to the right of each centre, fixes which way each page faces, for if Leviticus were turned over its calendar would fall two places to the left. Two points fix a line. One line does not prove five columns, but it settles how the three pages are to be set on one another, and everything that follows is read on that setting.

Set out this way, Leviticus tells us something about the columns of Exodus. Leviticus’s three rings each carry a mark of their own. The outer ring, chapters 1–7 and 20–27, names the place of revelation, the tent of meeting or Mount Sinai, at the edges of its units. The middle ring is marked by time, “seven days … on the eighth day.” The inner ring, round chapter 19, is marked by the person and his relations, the long lists of relatives in its laws. Exodus’s two outer columns fall on the ring of time, and its two inner columns on the ring of the person. Part C read the page’s scale from one man at the left to all Israel at the right. The lower books say that the two outer columns, the one man and the whole people, are the columns of time, the seven days and the year; and that the two inner columns are the columns of the person, his life and his neighbour.

Corners and Middles

How is a matching like this to be tested? Not by counting shared words. A unit’s words are the words of its kind: the sacrifices of Leviticus 1 share a vocabulary with the offerings of Numbers 28 whichever column each stands in, and a law code shares words with every other law code. Throughout the Torah the units that have proved to be the marks of a structure are the ones in its marked positions, its corners and its middles. In Genesis the four corners of the weave carry the sister-wife crises and its two centres the covenant and the ladder. In Leviticus each column has a middle unit, and in the first half of the book it is the middle unit of each column that lacks the mark of its ring. The test, then, is whether the marked positions of the three books, set in a column, hold the same subject.

Exodus has a complication of its own, and it turns out to be a help. Its columns are columns of four, with no single middle. Where Leviticus and Numbers have one unit in the middle of a column, Exodus has two, its inner pair, one from the story and one from the dwelling. Part C read those inner pairs as opposites that complete each other. Here they stand together, and the question is whether the two of them answer the one middle unit below.

The First Column

Take the first column, at the left. At its top Exodus has the bush: “do not come near here; take your sandals off your feet, for the place on which you stand is holy ground” (3:5). At the top of the column in Leviticus are chapters 8–10: the consecration of Aaron and his sons carried out, and then Nadab and Abihu, who “brought near before YHWH strange fire, which he had not commanded them, and fire came out from before YHWH and consumed them” (Leviticus 10:1–2). At the top of the column in Numbers the Levites are set round the dwelling, and “the stranger who comes near shall be put to death” (Numbers 1:51). Three tops, one subject: the boundary of the holy, and the man who may not come near it.

In the middle of the column Exodus has the signs and the plan of the dwelling. In the signs YHWH first draws a line between his people and Egypt: “I will set apart on that day the land of Goshen” (8:18), “that YHWH distinguishes between Egypt and Israel” (11:7). The plan sets out what is to be made holy and kept apart, the ark, the table, the lamp, the veil “between the holy and the holy of holies” (26:33). Leviticus’s middle unit in this column is chapter 11, the animals that may and may not be eaten, which ends: “to distinguish between the impure and the pure” (11:47). Numbers’ middle unit is chapters 5–6, which opens with the impure sent out of the camp, “that they not defile their camps, in whose midst I dwell” (5:3). Distinction, the pure kept from the impure, all down the column.

At the foot of the column Exodus has the consecration of the priests and the altar, seven days (29:35–37). Leviticus has chapter 12, the newborn: seven days, “and on the eighth day the flesh of his foreskin shall be circumcised” (12:3). Numbers has the dedication of the altar, “on the day Moses finished setting up the dwelling, he anointed it and consecrated it and all its vessels” (7:1), the princes’ gifts over twelve days, and the Levites presented for their service (8). The three are not one rite. In Exodus it is the priests and the altar; in Leviticus a child; in Numbers the altar again, and the Levites, over twelve days rather than seven. What they share is that each is the rite by which someone or something passes into the holy and begins its service there, and in the first two the passage is counted in the same way, seven days and then the eighth. Three feet, one subject: entry into the holy.

The Last Column

Now the last column, at the right. At its top Exodus has all the people at the mountain, bounded: “whoever touches the mountain shall surely be put to death; no hand shall touch him, but he shall surely be stoned” (19:12–13). Leviticus has chapter 24, the blasphemer, taken outside the camp “and all the congregation shall stone him” (24:14, 23). Numbers has the second census of the whole nation and Joshua set “over the congregation” (27:16). This is the least exact of the nine positions, and it should be stated no more widely than the texts allow. Exodus 19 does not speak of a congregation; it speaks of the people, fenced round the mountain as one body and bound by one penalty. Leviticus 24 has the whole congregation carry out that penalty with its own hands. Numbers 26–27 counts the whole body a second time and sets one man at its head. What the three share is the people treated as a single whole: bounded, acting, counted.

In the middle Exodus has the calendar of Unit 9 and the dwelling made by all the wise-hearted, “and the dwelling was one” (36:13); Leviticus has the calendar of chapter 23; Numbers has the calendar of chapters 28–29. This is the festival line that fixed the matching.

At the foot Exodus has the dwelling raised “on the first day of the first month” (40:2, 17), the glory filling it, and the cloud over it “before the eyes of all the house of Israel in all their journeys” (40:38). Leviticus has 22:26–33, the law of the newborn animal, “seven days under its mother, and from the eighth day on” (22:27), and its close: “I will be sanctified in the midst of the children of Israel; I am YHWH who sanctifies you, who brought you out of the land of Egypt to be your deity” (22:32–33). Numbers has the dated journeys: “these are the journeys of the children of Israel … they journeyed from Rameses in the first month, on the fifteenth day of the first month” (33:1–3). This foot is held firmly by Exodus and Numbers, which share the dated first month, the raising and the journeys. Leviticus’s unit here contributes less to it: the eighth-day law, and a closing formula that answers the close of the consecration (29:45–46) rather than Unit 19. So the last column holds its subject, the people as one body in its own time, at its top and its middle in all three books, and at its foot in two of them, with Leviticus answering by its formula.

The Middle Column

The middle column is the three single units. At the centre of Exodus, the covenant: “Moses alone shall come near to YHWH, and they shall not come near” (24:2); and yet the nobles of the children of Israel “beheld the deity, and ate and drank” (24:11). At the centre of Leviticus: “you shall be holy, for I YHWH your deity am holy” (19:2), said to “all the congregation of the children of Israel.” At the centre of Numbers: Korah and his company gathered against Moses and Aaron, “all the congregation are holy, every one of them, and YHWH is in their midst” (16:3), and the earth opens; then the plague, stopped by Aaron, who “stood between the dead and the living” (17:13). The unit before it ends “one law and one judgment shall be for you and for the stranger” (15:16); the unit after it opens “the stranger who comes near shall be put to death” (18:7). Who may come near, and the holiness that all claim: this is what the middle column carries through the three books. In Exodus it is given to one man and glimpsed by seventy; in Leviticus it is commanded to all; in Numbers it is claimed by all, and the claim is answered.

The Two Inner Columns

The two inner columns are another matter. Leviticus has a middle unit in each of them: chapter 17 in the second column, “the life of the flesh is in the blood, and I have given it to you upon the altar to make atonement for your lives” (17:11), and chapter 21 in the fourth, the priests and the dead. Exodus has its inner pairs, the Passover and the garments in the second column, the cases of Unit 8 and the gifts in the fourth. But Numbers’ two short columns hold two units each and have no middle at all. Where one book lacks a marked unit, the other lacks it too, and the columns cannot be tested there as the outer ones can. The tops still answer. In the second column the Passover’s blood on the doors, “and there shall be no plague upon you” (12:13), stands over Leviticus 16, the blood brought within the veil “once in the year” (16:34), a phrase these two books use only here and at Exodus 30:10, in the same column. In the fourth, the waters of Meribah in Exodus 17 stand over the waters of Meribah in Numbers 20, the only two Meribahs in the Torah’s middle books, both with the rock and the staff. But these are threads, not marks.

Marked-Position Pattern: the alignment stands where it can be tested, at the two outer columns and the centre, and the subjects that run down them are the ones the first column of Exodus announced at the bush and the last at the mountain: the boundary of the holy, and the congregation bounded round it.

The Borders of Sinai

One more thing runs through the three books, and it is not a column. It is a frame.

The unit before Israel reaches Sinai is Jethro’s visit, Unit 7. He comes to Moses “in the wilderness where he was camped, the mountain of the deity” (18:5), sees him judging the people from morning to evening, and asks, “why do you sit alone … you will surely wear away, you and this people with you, for the thing is too heavy for you; you cannot do it alone” (18:14, 18). He tells Moses to choose men of ability, “and they shall bear with you” (18:22). That is the first row of Unit 7. In its second row the people arrive at Sinai, “in the third month” (19:1), and the pericope opens. Jethro’s counsel stands in the cell before the door.

It closes in the same words. When the camp strikes from Sinai, Moses asks Hobab, Jethro’s kin, to come with them (Numbers 10:29–32), and in the first unit after the departure Moses says what Jethro said: “I am not able to carry all this people alone, for it is too heavy for me” (11:14). YHWH answers with Jethro’s counsel: “gather me seventy men of the elders of Israel … and they shall bear with you the burden of the people, and you shall not bear it alone” (11:16–17). “They shall bear with you,” ונשאו אתך, occurs in Exodus and Numbers only in these two places, and in each book it stands in the same cell of its unit. The pericope itself closes when the cloud lifts, “in the second year, in the second month, on the twentieth of the month” (Numbers 10:11), in the last unit of Numbers’ first column; the seventy elders come in the unit after that. So the two borders stand just outside the pericope, one cell before it opens and one unit after it closes, like the two posts of a door. Between them lies the whole of the second book of Exodus, the whole of Leviticus, and the first column of Numbers.

On the map the two border units are the top right unit of Exodus and the top of the second column of Numbers, both in the top row of their book. They are not in one column; they stand on either side of everything the pericope holds, and both are outside it. The columns read above run inside this frame. That is worth keeping in mind when the vertical arm of the cross is called a close-up of the forty years: the author has drawn its edges himself, with the one visitor who comes from outside, and the counsel he gives.

The Three Single Units

Part A set three units apart because they belong to no pair: the sea, the covenant and the calf. On the page they stand in one column, the middle one, and Part C read them as a division, a joining and a connection. They have one more thing to show, and it is only visible on the map of the whole Torah.

Each of the three horizontal books has one single unit of its own. In Genesis it is Babel, Unit 4, nine verses standing between the opening triad and the fathers. In Leviticus it is chapter 19, Unit 13, at the centre of the rings. In Deuteronomy it is the last unit, Unit 13, Moses blessing the tribes and dying on the mountain. Exodus, with its three single units in one column, answers all three.

The sea answers Babel. At Babel the whole earth is of one speech, and the builders say, “let us make a name for ourselves, lest we be scattered over the face of all the earth” (Genesis 11:4); YHWH comes down, confuses the speech, “and from there YHWH scattered them over the face of all the earth” (11:8–9). At the sea the people who were one house in Egypt go out of it, and “Israel saw Egypt dead on the shore of the sea” (14:30). Both are places where YHWH turns the course of history by dividing: the nations from each other at Babel, Israel from Egypt at the sea. Both units are small and stand alone, and in each the division is the whole event.

The calf answers the end of Deuteronomy. Both are five rows of two, the only such pair of single units in the Torah. In Exodus 33 “YHWH spoke to Moses face to face, as a man speaks to his friend” (33:11); Deuteronomy ends, “no prophet has arisen since in Israel like Moses, whom YHWH knew face to face” (34:10). In both Moses stands between YHWH and the people, and faces the people with something on his face: the veil he puts on after speaking with YHWH (34:33), and the blessing he gives the tribes “before his death” (Deuteronomy 33:1). Moses facing the people, veiled and blessing them: the connection between above and below, made through one man.

And the covenant answers chapter 19 of Leviticus, which lies directly beneath it on the map. These two are the centres of their books. At the one, the people answer with one voice and the blood of the covenant is put on the altar and on them (24:3, 6–8); at the other, holiness is commanded to the whole congregation (19:2), with “I am YHWH” said after law after law.

Here the vertical arm adds a third centre, for Numbers too has one, Korah, in the middle of the camp. Set the three centres of the vertical books side by side. In Exodus, “Moses came into the midst of the cloud and went up the mountain” (24:18): a man goes up into the cloud. In Numbers, when the congregation gathers against Moses and Aaron, “they turned toward the tent of meeting, and the cloud covered it, and the glory of YHWH appeared” (17:7): the cloud comes down into the camp. Between them, in Leviticus, no one goes up and nothing comes down. The people are told to be what YHWH is: “you shall be holy, for I … am holy.” The three centres of the arm of place are a going up, an imitation, and a coming down. The three single units of Exodus, standing in a line down the middle of its page, hold the whole cross in small: Babel, the dwelling, and the death of Moses answered by the sea, the covenant and the calf, with the covenant at the crossing, over the one chapter of Leviticus that every other chapter surrounds.

Single-Unit Pattern: the sea answers Babel, the calf answers the end of Deuteronomy, the covenant answers Leviticus 19; and the three centres of the vertical books are a going up, an imitation, and a coming down.

Consecration Flows Down

The columns of the three books run down, and so do their rows. Counting the covenant’s row as the seam between the two books of Exodus, as Part B treated it, Exodus has four rows of units, Leviticus three and Numbers five: twelve rows from the top of the first book to the end of the third.

There is a progression, and it is the one thing the three books are about. It can be followed in a single phrase. “I dwell in their midst,” said of YHWH, occurs in the three vertical books four times, and the four step down the rows. The first two are in Exodus, in its two lower rows: “let them make me a sanctuary, and I will dwell in their midst” (25:8), at the head of the plan; and “I will dwell in the midst of the children of Israel, and I will be their deity” (29:45), at the close of the consecration. The sanctuary is where YHWH dwells. The third is in Numbers, in the middle row of the camp, the row that holds the dwelling: the impure are to be sent out of the camp, “that they not defile their camps, in whose midst I dwell” (5:3). Now it is the camp. The fourth is in the row below that, in the last unit of the book: “you shall not defile the land in which you dwell, in whose midst I dwell, for I YHWH dwell in the midst of the children of Israel” (35:34). Now it is the land. The sanctuary, the camp, the land: the place in whose midst YHWH dwells widens as the rows go down.

The verb “to sanctify” goes the same way, and it shows who is being made holy at each step. In Exodus’s lower rows it is the house and its priests: “you shall anoint them and fill their hand and sanctify them, and they shall serve me as priests” (28:41); “the tent of meeting shall be sanctified by my glory, and I will sanctify the tent of meeting and the altar, and Aaron and his sons I will sanctify to serve me as priests” (29:43–44); and on the day the dwelling is raised, “you shall anoint the dwelling and all that is in it, and sanctify it and all its vessels, and it shall be holy” (40:9). In Leviticus’s first row the anointing is carried out (8:10–11). In its second row the priest is sanctified in his person: “you shall sanctify him, for he offers the bread of your deity; he shall be holy to you, for I YHWH who sanctify you am holy” (21:8), “I am YHWH who sanctifies him” (21:15). In its third row it is the people: “I will be sanctified in the midst of the children of Israel; I am YHWH who sanctifies you” (22:32). Then Numbers, and here the movement is no longer row by row. The camp page is read inward, from its edges to the dwelling at its centre, and the verb is spread across it. What changes is its object. Nothing inside the house is sanctified any more, except once, when the dwelling and the altar are anointed on the day they are set up and the Levites are given with them (7:1; 8). What is sanctified now is the people themselves, told to “sanctify yourselves for tomorrow” (11:18); the camp, kept clean for the one who dwells in it (5:3); the censers of Korah’s company, which came near, and are “holy” (17:2); and at the second Meribah YHWH himself, who “was sanctified in them” (20:13), in the people, at the waters where they strove with him. These are not stages in an order; they are places on the camp page, and what they have in common is their object. In Exodus the people sanctify the house. In Numbers YHWH is sanctified in the people.

So the three books carry consecration downward and outward: from the house to its priests and from the priests to the people, row by row, in Exodus and Leviticus; and then, in Numbers, no longer in order but spread across the camp: the people, the camp they keep, the land they will hold, and YHWH sanctified in them. The widening is in the object, not in the row. What fills the dwelling at the end of Exodus, the glory, is what dwells in the midst of the land at the end of Numbers. Leviticus, between them, is where the holiness of the house passes into the holiness of the people who stand round it. The vertical arm of the cross is a line of place, as I said at the start. Read by its rows, it is the line along which the holy spreads: a sanctuary, then a camp round it, then a land.

From the Names of the Sons to the House of Israel

Now the second of Part C’s questions. What does it mean that Exodus begins with the names of the sons and ends with the house? Exodus opens, “these are the names of the sons of Israel who came to Egypt with Jacob, each man and his house” (1:1), and it ends with “all the house of Israel” watching the cloud on the dwelling “in all their journeys” (40:38). Between the one line and the other, the sons of Israel have become the house of Israel, and the thing they watch is the glory of YHWH. Those are the two matters of the book, and the book is built so that they cannot be told apart.

Genesis left both of them open. It ended with Elohim dominant and YHWH silent. Joseph, who speaks of Elohim and never of YHWH, closes the book with an oath: “Elohim will surely remember you, and you shall carry my bones up from here” (Genesis 50:25). The sons of Israel are in Egypt, seventy souls, each man with his house, under a brother who has organized the land so that “all the earth came to Egypt” (41:57). Egypt, as Joseph made it, is the fullest expression in the Torah of Elohim’s blessing to Adam: be fruitful, fill the earth, rule it. The first verse of Exodus’s story says the blessing has been kept: “the children of Israel were fruitful and swarmed and multiplied and grew very strong, and the land was filled with them” (1:7). What Genesis did not say is what the name YHWH, silent since Jacob left Laban, has to do with these people. Exodus takes that up as its first business. At the bush Moses asks what he is to answer when the people ask, “what is his name?” The answer is given twice, first “I will be what I will be” and then “YHWH, the deity of your fathers” (3:13–15). In Egypt it is given again, in the one verse where the two names stand together as speaker and name: “Elohim spoke to Moses and said to him, I am YHWH” (6:2). And it is given to the people as a promise with two halves: “I will take you to me as a people, and I will be to you a deity, and you shall know that I am YHWH your deity who brings you out from under the burdens of Egypt” (6:7).

That verse, I think, is the book in one sentence. The text does not say so; what the text does is repeat it, at the close of the consecration, in the other book: “I will dwell in the midst of the children of Israel, and I will be their deity, and they shall know that I am YHWH their deity, who brought them out of the land of Egypt to dwell in their midst” (29:45–46). The two statements stand in the same column, in the story and in the dwelling, and they say the same two things in the same order: Israel becomes YHWH’s people, and Israel comes to know YHWH’s name. In the first, the knowledge is to come from the going out. In the second, it comes from the dwelling in their midst. The recurrence is the text’s. What follows is my reading of it. Everything the page showed is in these two verses. Across the page, from one man to all Israel, is the making of a people. Down the page, from the sons of Israel to the one dwelling, is the making of a house for YHWH. The two directions are one promise.

This is why the book “presents itself as two books,” as Part A put it, and why the two are not two. The story brings the people out so that they may know the name; the dwelling brings the name into the midst of the people. Leaving Joseph’s Egypt is the first step of both. Joseph’s bones go out with the people at the sea (13:19), under the oath sworn to Elohim; and in the same row of the same unit YHWH goes before them in the pillar of cloud (13:21). Elohim’s blessing is not revoked; it is carried out of Egypt. What Egypt could not hold, and what the sea is crossed for, is the other name, and the book is the account of how that name comes to dwell among the people who carried the blessing out.

The names on stage say so, at the places where the page is marked. Part C showed that in the middle column Elohim acts at the two ends, at the sea and at the calf, and not at the centre, where the one who cuts the covenant is YHWH. Separation and connection, the pattern of the second and fifth days, are Elohim’s; the covenant between them is YHWH’s term, the one that Elohim’s creation does not have. The corners say the same thing another way. At the bush and at the finished dwelling the holy presence fills a place and Moses stands outside it; at the mountain and at the consecration one stands for the many before it. In all four corners the question is who may come near, and the three books below the page carry that question down their middle column, from Moses alone, through “you shall be holy,” to Korah. The whole vertical arm is the working out of the two halves of 6:7: a people, and a name known in its midst.

Conclusion: What the Architecture Does

What the architecture of Exodus means, then, is what it does. Its page makes a people out of one man’s commission and a house out of the sons of Israel, and it sets the covenant where the two makings cross. Its three single units carry the cross of the Torah in small. Its columns run on into Leviticus and Numbers at the positions that the author marks everywhere, the corners and the middles. And its rows, with the rows of the two books under it, carry the holiness of the house down into the people, the camp and the land. The sons of Israel came to Egypt each man with his house. At the end of Exodus they are one house, and YHWH is in its midst.

Where the Pattern Is Thin

  • The two inner columns are not tested by their middles. Numbers’ second and fourth columns have two units each and no middle unit, so the three-book test of corners and middles applies only to the first column, the last and the centre. The second column runs firmly from Exodus into Leviticus (the blood, the ransom, “once in the year,” “that he not die”) and only thinly into Numbers, where it holds two stories of death in the camp and a plea for pardon. The fourth column holds in pieces: the two Meribahs, a capital law shared word for word between Exodus 21:17 and Leviticus 20:9, the freewill gift of Exodus 35 and Leviticus 22, Aaron’s garments made in Exodus 39 and passed to Eleazar in Numbers 20. I have not given it a name, because I do not think it has one yet.
  • Some links cross the columns. The cloud that covers the dwelling at the end of Exodus (40:34–38) is taken up at the start of Numbers (9:15–23), in the opposite corner of the page. The count of 603,550 that closes Exodus (38:26) opens Numbers (1:46). The daily lamb of Exodus 29:38–42 returns in Numbers 28:3–8, in another column. These look like the seams where one book is sewn to the next rather than columns, but they are real, and a reader who finds them should not be told they are not there.
  • The first column’s two story units hold their column by details. The call at the bush and the nine signs belong to the first column by the boundary of the holy (3:5) and the distinction YHWH makes (8:18; 11:7). Their main events, the call and the plagues, are not about the sanctuary.
  • The three single units against the horizontal books. The sea answers Babel and the calf answers the end of Deuteronomy by shape and by one event each; the covenant answers Leviticus 19 by position. These are three matches, not a rule, and I have not looked for the smaller single units of the other books.
  • The twelve rows share a shape, not a content, row for row. Numbers’ five rows have the five-part shape Part C found in the author’s structures: the road at the top and the outside at the bottom, the tribes counted and the tribes moved and settled on either side of the middle, and holiness between the two vows at the centre. But Exodus runs down from the people to YHWH’s dwelling, and Numbers runs inward from the outside to the holy centre. What answers across them is the phrase I followed, and the top rows, which are the road, with Jethro at both edges.

What Comes Next

The introduction to Exodus is complete. Part A found the units; Part B built the page; Part C read its columns and rows; this part set the page in the Torah. The reader now has the map, and the map is what the commentaries on the nineteen units will use. Each unit will be read in its place: in its pair, in its block, in its row and column, and, where its column runs on, against the unit that stands under it in Leviticus or Numbers. The first commentary will be on Unit 1, the bush, where one man is told not to come near, and asks what name he is to give.
