This is where we are going. Exodus has nineteen units, and this is how they lie: four quarters of four units each, and three units that stand alone between them. The four parts of this introduction show how the map is found and how it is read.

Where Exodus Stands

The Torah has five books, and they are not five in a row.

Each book is made of units, stretches of text that begin and end in places the text itself marks. Part A will show how they are found. Three of the books are built alike. Genesis, Leviticus and Deuteronomy each set their units out in three rows, and the same plan can be followed from one to the next. Call that the thread that runs across.

Exodus and Numbers are built another way. But they have something else in common, and they share it with Leviticus. A story begins in Exodus, stops at a mountain, and starts again in Numbers. A tabernacle is built in Exodus, served all through Leviticus, and packed up to travel at the beginning of Numbers. Call that the thread that runs down. It is a thread of subject, to begin with. Part D will show that there is more to it than that.

Leviticus is where the two threads cross. Exodus stands above it, at the head of the thread that runs down. All five books, with every one of their eighty-six units, are laid out on the map of the whole Torah at chaver.com.

So Exodus is the first book we come to that isn’t built in threes. It has a plan of its own, and this introduction is about finding it.

One Book, Seen from Two Sides

Genesis ends in Egypt, with a family. Exodus begins there, with the same family’s names: “These are the names of the sons of Israel who came to Egypt” (1:1). By the end of the book they are “all the house of Israel” (40:38), camped around a tent with a cloud on it.

You can tell what happens in between from either side.

For Israel, this is a story of redemption. They are slaves, and they are brought out.

For YHWH, it is a revelation. Over and over in this book something is done “so that you may know that I am YHWH.”

The two are said together in one verse, before the first plague: “I will take you to me for a people, and I will be your deity, and you shall know that I am YHWH your deity, who brings you out from under the burdens of Egypt” (6:7).

A people is made, and YHWH is made known.

You might think that sentence was finished at the sea. It isn’t. It comes back in the middle of the instructions for the tabernacle, with something added: “I will dwell in the midst of the children of Israel, and I will be their deity. And they shall know that I am YHWH their deity, who brought them out of the land of Egypt to dwell in their midst” (29:45–46).

They are brought out so that he may dwell among them. The redemption ends with a people building a house, and the revelation ends with YHWH in it.

Exodus doesn’t tell those as two stories. How it holds them together is what its architecture shows.

What Is Different About This Book

In Genesis and Leviticus the surprise is that there is a map at all. In Exodus there is a second surprise.

Three of its units stand alone. Around them the book falls into four parts, and each of the four falls into four units. And the four units of a part turn out to stand to one another the way the four parts of the book do. The whole can be seen again, smaller, in each of its quarters.

You can see it on the map: four squares, and each of them a square of four. None of the other four books does that. It is the thing to watch for.

The Four Parts of This Introduction

Part A, The Units of Exodus, asks where the book breaks. Printed Bibles give it forty chapters. Its own seams give it nineteen units. We find them in the open: first the five places where the book changes its voice, and then the units between, one stretch at a time. When they are all found they fall four, one, four, one, four, one, four. Nobody was looking for that.

Part B, The Map of Exodus, begins with the oddest thing about the book: it is a story for twenty-four chapters and a building for sixteen. It sets the nineteen units out as a map, four quarters with three units standing alone between them, and reads the quarters one at a time. Each does what the whole book does. And at the four corners of the map there are four holy places, each with a line around it.

Part C, Columns and Rows, reads the same map along its long lines. Across it, from one side to the other, the book goes from one man at a bush to “all the house of Israel.” Down it, from top to bottom, YHWH comes from a mountain into the midst of the people, and the wonders turn into something else.

Part D, Architecture and Meaning in Exodus, follows the map out of the book. Both halves of Exodus lead out of it. The story goes on in Numbers and the tabernacle in Leviticus, and the three books turn out to be laid out the way Israel camps. Then the columns of the map are followed down into the next book.

After that the units are taken one at a time, each with a commentary of its own.

A Word on the Approach

This is a reading of the Hebrew text as it stands. It does not depend on deciding who wrote the Torah, or when. Whether you read Exodus as scripture or as an ancient book, you can follow the same words and examine the same arrangements.

We begin with how Exodus is made and ask what that brings into view. The text is there for you to check the reading against.

Part A: The Units of Exodus

A Stage-by-Stage Discovery

Where Does It Break?

Open any printed Exodus and it has been cut up for you into forty chapters. The Torah has no chapters. They were laid over the text many centuries after it was written, and they don’t always fall where its own divisions do.

So where does the Torah itself end one piece and begin the next?

Genesis tells you. “These are the generations of,” it says, again and again, and you know something new has started. Exodus has nothing like that. Its favourite sentence is “YHWH spoke to Moses, saying,” and you might hope that would do. It won’t. In the plan of the tabernacle that sentence opens a whole unit, and then a section inside the unit, and then a single instruction about a basin. It tells you someone is about to speak, and that’s all it tells you.

But the book does show its seams, if you know what to look for. Sometimes it stops writing one kind of thing and starts writing another. Sometimes the words a piece began with come back to close it. Sometimes a piece has a design of its own that runs from one end of it to the other. And sometimes you know a piece only because you have found the one before it and the one after.

We’ll start with the places nobody can miss, and work inward from there.

One thing has to be clear first. Passages can belong together without being one unit. A unit has two things at once: edges, and a single design that fills everything between them. We are going to meet stretches that anyone would call one thing, and that turn out to be two units, because each half has a build and a close of its own. And we are going to meet episodes that look separate and turn out to be one unit, because one build runs through all of them. So at each line we draw, look for both: what happens at the edge, and how the inside is made.

Five Places Where the Book Changes Its Voice

Read Exodus straight through, and five times you can feel the ground change under you.

The first is at the mountain. For twenty chapters you have been told a story. Then: “These are the judgments that you shall set before them” (21:1). And you are no longer hearing what happened. You are hearing what is to be done when a man strikes his neighbour, when an ox gores, when somebody leaves a pit open.

Two chapters on, the law stops. Its last word is an odd one, about not boiling a kid in its mother’s milk (23:19). It is a law the Torah uses to close a series of laws. And the next sentence isn’t a law at all: “Behold, I send a messenger before you, to guard you on the way and to bring you to the place I have prepared” (23:20). The same voice is speaking, but now it is talking about a road and a country, and it leads straight into the making of the covenant at the foot of the mountain.

Then Moses walks into the cloud and is gone forty days. And the voice begins again, with a shopping list: gold, silver and bronze; blue, purple and scarlet yarn; linen, goats’ hair, skins, acacia wood, oil, spices, stones (25:1–7). “Let them make me a sanctuary, and I will dwell in their midst” (25:8). For nearly seven chapters after that nothing else is talked about.

The list ends as suddenly as it began. “And he gave to Moses, when he had finished speaking with him on Mount Sinai, the two tablets of the testimony” (31:18). The story is back. Unfortunately it is the story of the golden calf.

And one last time: “Moses assembled all the congregation of the children of Israel and said to them, These are the things that YHWH has commanded you to do” (35:1). From here to the end of the book, everything that was ordered on the mountain is made.

The law of the Passover is given in the middle of the night the people leave Egypt, and the Ten Words are spoken at the mountain. That’s true. But those are set inside a story that goes on around them. After the law of the Passover comes midnight, and the firstborn of Egypt (12:29). After the Ten Words the people are standing far off, terrified. At the five flags nothing goes on. One thing stops, and something else takes its place.

The Story

So begin with what anyone can catch.

The book opens with a list of names. “These are the names of the sons of Israel who came to Egypt” (1:1): Reuben, Simeon, Levi, and so on. Six chapters later, when Pharaoh has said no and things have gone from bad to worse, the story stops dead for another list. “These are the heads of their fathers’ houses” (6:14). Reuben again, then Simeon, then Levi. And at Levi it slows down and goes from father to son, until it reaches the two men it has been looking for: “These are the Aaron and Moses to whom YHWH said, Bring the children of Israel out of the land of Egypt” (6:26).

A list of names at the start, and the same list begun over in chapter 6. Whatever lies between them belongs together.

The second thing is even easier. Everyone knows there were ten plagues. Nobody would think of cutting them in half.

That gives us two large pieces before we have done any fine work at all: from the first list of names to the second, and from the first plague to the last.

Each of them is two units. And that takes a closer look.

The names

Between the two lists there is a great deal of story. A new king sets the people to hard labour and orders their sons thrown into the river. One son is put into the river in a basket and pulled out by the king’s daughter. He grows up, kills an Egyptian, and runs for his life to Midian (2:15). There he marries, keeps sheep, and one day turns aside to look at a bush.

When the voice from the bush has finished with him, he goes home to his father-in-law and asks leave to go back to his brothers in Egypt. Jethro says, “Go in peace” (4:18).

And there the first unit ends. Moses ran to Midian, and now Midian lets him go.

You might have put the line somewhere else. Why not where he runs away, or where he comes to the bush? Because of how the unit is made. Three times over, it sets the people beside the one man. His flight and the bush both fall inside that design.

In Midian. We knew where he was. The text says it anyway, and a few verses later it sends a second order to match the first: “YHWH said to Aaron, Go to meet Moses in the wilderness” (4:27).

The brothers meet, gather the elders, and go in to Pharaoh. Pharaoh has never heard of YHWH and sees no reason to start now. He takes away the straw. The people, who believed Moses a chapter ago, stop listening to him.

Then comes the second list of names. And after it a verse that names a place once more: “And it was, on the day YHWH spoke to Moses in the land of Egypt” (6:28).

Read straight on, that sounds like the start of the next sentence. The Hebrew text sets a paragraph break after it all the same. And what follows begins from the top, as if nothing had yet been said: “YHWH spoke to Moses, saying, I am YHWH; speak to Pharaoh king of Egypt all that I speak to you” (6:29).

The unit began by naming a place. At its far edge a place is named again.

So the two lists of names close off two units, and the line between them is drawn with the name of a place.

Nine, and one

Why aren’t the ten plagues one unit? Because the text closes the account at nine.

It begins with that fresh start, and with Moses objecting as he has objected before: “I am of uncircumcised lips; how will Pharaoh listen to me?” (6:30). He said the same thing just before the list of names (6:12). It didn’t work then either.

Then, before anything has happened: “Moses and Aaron did as YHWH commanded them; so they did” (7:6).

A staff turns into a serpent. The river turns to blood. Frogs, lice, swarms, the cattle, boils, hail, locusts, darkness. Count them: nine. A tenth is promised: “One more plague I will bring on Pharaoh and on Egypt” (11:1).

It doesn’t come. What comes is this: “Moses and Aaron did all these wonders before Pharaoh” (11:10).

They “did as YHWH commanded” at the start, and they “did all these wonders” at the end. The account is closed, with nine plagues done and the tenth still hanging.

The tenth gets a beginning of its own. “YHWH said to Moses and to Aaron in the land of Egypt” (12:1).

We have heard that before. The second unit opened by saying where YHWH spoke: in Midian. This one opens by saying where: in the land of Egypt. Both times a place is named at the line that divides a pair.

What follows is one night, told two ways at once: as it happened, and as it is to be kept ever after. “I will pass through the land of Egypt on this night” (12:12). And in the same breath: “This day shall be for you a remembrance, and you shall keep it as a feast to YHWH for your generations” (12:14). At midnight the firstborn of Egypt die, and the people are hurried out with their dough before it can rise.

One verse holds both nights together. “It is a night of watching for YHWH, to bring them out of the land of Egypt; this night is YHWH’s, a watching for all the children of Israel for their generations” (12:42).

The unit keeps saying when. “On this very day” (בעצם היום הזה) is found three times in Exodus, and all three are here (12:17, 41, 51). It ends looking a long way ahead, to a son who will one day ask what it all means (13:14), and a sign to wear on the hand (13:16).

Twice now, something anyone can see as a whole has turned out to be two units: the names, and the ten plagues. Two cases prove nothing. But they raise a possibility, that units may come in pairs, and Part B will take it up.¹

The sea

The night is over and the people are out. The story now begins again from Pharaoh’s side: “And it was, when Pharaoh sent the people out” (13:17).

You know what happens. It ends with Miriam and her drum, and the women dancing (15:21). And the very next thing we are told is that everyone is walking away: “Moses made Israel journey from the Sea of Reeds” (15:22).

So the sea has an edge on each side. Before it is the night, which ended with the sign on the hand. After it is the road.

It has a small mark of its own as well. When it opens, the fear is that the people will “see war” and run back to Egypt (13:17). In the song at its end, it is YHWH who is “a man of war” (15:3).

Three stops

Now the people are on the road, and the road has a rhythm. They journey, they arrive, and they complain.

They journey from the Sea of Reeds into the wilderness of Shur, and the water is bitter (15:22). They journey from Elim to the wilderness of Sin, and there is no bread (16:1). They journey on to Rephidim, and there is no water at all (17:1).

That might be three little units, one to a stop. But one word runs through all three, and at the third stop it turns round.

At the first: “there he set for them a statute and a judgment, and there he tested them” (15:25). At the second YHWH sends bread from the sky, “that I may test them, whether they will walk in my teaching or not” (16:4). At the third Moses loses patience: “Why do you test YHWH?” (17:2). The place is named Massah, Testing, “because they tested YHWH, saying, Is YHWH in our midst or not?” (17:7).

Twice he tests them. The third time they test him.

That shows the three stops belong together. By now you know that isn’t enough. The ten plagues belong together too, and they are two units.

What settles it is the build. The plagues were closed after the ninth, and the tenth was given an opening of its own. Here nothing is closed and nothing is opened. Each stop is told in the same two steps: the complaint and its answer, and then what follows at that place. The three are laid one under another, and that is the whole unit.

A visit, and the mountain

The last unit of this stretch begins with someone arriving. “Jethro, Moses’ father-in-law, came with his sons and his wife to Moses, into the wilderness where he was camped, at the mountain of the deity” (18:5).

What is a family visit doing in the same unit as the thunder at Sinai? Look at what the visit leaves behind.

Jethro finds his son-in-law sitting from morning to evening, judging every quarrel in Israel by himself. He tells him it can’t go on. The small matters should go to judges. Moses should keep one task: “You be for the people before the deity, and bring the matters to the deity. And you shall warn them of the statutes and the teachings” (18:19–20). Moses takes the advice, and judges are appointed.

Then the people come to the mountain, and the text makes an occasion of it. “In the third month after the children of Israel had gone out of the land of Egypt, on that day they came to the wilderness of Sinai” (19:1). “They journeyed from Rephidim … and Israel camped there, facing the mountain” (19:2).

A date and a journey. Those are just the things we have been watching for, and something new does begin here. So why isn’t it a new unit?

Because the new scene sits inside an account that has already begun, and Jethro began it. Follow Moses.

At the mountain YHWH gives him words for the people. He carries them down, and “Moses brought the words of the people back to YHWH” (19:8). YHWH tells him why it is being done this way: “so that the people may hear when I speak with you, and believe in you for ever” (19:9).

On the third day the mountain shakes, and “Moses spoke, and the deity answered him with a voice” (19:19).

Then the Ten Words are spoken, to everyone, and it is more than they can stand. They say to Moses, “You speak with us, and we will hear; but let not Elohim speak with us, or we shall die” (20:15 [English 20:19]). And so it is: “The people stood far off, and Moses drew near to the thick darkness where the deity was” (20:17 [English 20:21]).

That is the place Jethro gave him. From the visit to the end, Moses stands between the people and the deity, and each scene puts him there again.

The Law, and the Covenant

The law is as plainly one thing as the ten plagues were.

It lies between two flags. It has one heading, “These are the judgments,” and no second heading anywhere. And it begins and ends on the same count. Its first law is about a slave: “six years he shall serve, and in the seventh he shall go out free” (21:2). Among its last is the land: “six years you shall sow your land … and in the seventh you shall let it rest” (23:10–11).

One heading, and a six and a seventh at each end. And once again it is two units. This time you can hear where it divides.

The first part is nearly all cases. “When a man …” “If …” A slave is due his freedom. One man kills another, or two men quarrel. An ox gores, a pit is left open, a fire gets into a neighbour’s field. Something left with a neighbour goes missing. It is the law of people who live close together and get in each other’s way.

Then: “A sorceress you shall not let live” (22:17 [English 22:18]).

There is no “when” and no “if” about it. It is an order, and nearly everything after it is an order.

This second part is very carefully made. It opens with three who are to have no place among the people: the sorceress, the man who lies with a beast, the one who sacrifices to other deities. It ends with the whole people and its calendar: a seventh year, a seventh day and three feasts. The two ends answer each other. At the start, lying with a beast is a capital crime. At the close, the seventh day is kept “so that your ox and your donkey may rest” (23:12).

And inside those two ends there is another pair. After the three outcasts the laws turn to the stranger, “for you were strangers in the land of Egypt” (22:20 [English 22:21]). They come back to him, in the same words, just before the calendar (23:9).

Paul Hocking and I have set this design out in full: five rows of three, rising from the single outsider to the nation and its year.²

The covenant

The law has ended with the kid in its mother’s milk. What comes after it is the promise of a messenger, and at first it looks like a tail on the law, a few encouraging words before the next thing.

It isn’t a tail. It is the first third of the most tightly built unit in the book.

It has three parts, and each of the three has three.

In the first, YHWH speaks to the whole people, and each of its three parts opens with something that goes ahead of them: “I send a messenger before you” (23:20); “my messenger will go before you” (23:23); “I will send the hornet before you” (23:28). (The middle part sends one thing more before it closes: “my terror,” 23:27.)

In the second, the scene moves partway up the mountain. Moses, Aaron, Nadab, Abihu and seventy elders are called to come up, and the people answer, “All the words that YHWH has spoken we will do” (24:1–3). The covenant is made, with an altar, a book and a basin of blood (24:4–8). Then the elders go up. “They saw the deity of Israel,” and they ate and drank (24:9–11).

In the third, Moses goes on alone. He is called up to receive the tablets (24:12–13). The elders are told to wait, and the cloud covers the mountain (24:14–15). The glory of YHWH rests on Sinai, and Moses walks into the cloud (24:16–18).

All the people are at the foot. A few are partway up. One man is at the top.

The Plan, and the Tablets

Moses is in the cloud, and for seven chapters one voice speaks and nobody answers. It is describing a tabernacle, piece by piece. Call it the plan.

Its first two units close themselves.

The first begins with something for Moses to pass on: “Speak to the children of Israel, that they take me an offering” (25:2). Then come an ark, a table and a lampstand; curtains, boards and bars; a veil and a screen; an altar and a court. Three times Moses is reminded that he has seen all this already, that he is to make it as it was “shown you on the mountain” (25:40; 26:30; 27:8). And then something for him to pass on again: “You shall command the children of Israel, that they take to you pure olive oil” (27:20).

The final verse of the unit gives the place the name it will have for the rest of the book. It calls it the tent of meeting (27:21).

The second turns from the house to the men who will serve in it, or rather to what they will wear. Holy garments for Aaron, “for glory and for beauty” (28:2). An ephod, a breastpiece, a robe hung with bells, a gold plate for his forehead. And garments for his sons, “for glory and for beauty” (28:40).

For once a unit and a chapter are the same thing.

The third and fourth are harder to pull apart, and the text helps by starting them the same way. “This is the thing that you shall do to them, to make them holy” (29:1). And later in the same chapter: “And this is what you shall do on the altar” (29:38).

What you do to them takes seven days. Aaron and his sons are washed, dressed and anointed, a bull and two rams are offered, and at the end of the week they are priests. Its last word is about the altar: “Seven days you shall make atonement for the altar and make it holy, and the altar shall be most holy; whoever touches the altar shall become holy” (29:37). The Hebrew text leaves a paragraph break there.

What you do on the altar never ends. A lamb every morning and a lamb every evening, “a continual burnt offering for your generations” (29:42). Incense every morning and every evening, “a continual incense before YHWH” (30:8). After those comes everything the daily round needs: half a shekel from every man who is counted, a basin to wash in, the oil, the incense, and two craftsmen to make it all.

The seven days make a priest. The service is what a priest then does, every day, without end. That is the line between the two units.

The stretch ends with the Sabbath, “a sign for ever” (31:17).

The next stretch is one unit, and it is held shut by the thing it is named for.

It closes with Moses coming down, “the two tablets of the testimony in Moses’ hand” (34:29), his face shining so that he has to wear a veil.

The Building

Moses comes down, gathers everybody, and the first thing he talks about is the Sabbath. “Six days work shall be done, but the seventh day shall be holy to you” (35:2).

We have just heard about the Sabbath. It was the last thing said on the mountain before the tablets were handed over. Now it is the first thing said after them.

Then he asks for gold. He reads out the list that opened the plan, and the people go off and come back carrying things. The women spin. The leaders bring the stones. Bezalel and Oholiab are put in charge.

And the unit ends with a problem builders seldom have. The craftsmen come to Moses and tell him to make it stop. “The material was enough for all the work, and too much” (36:7).

Then the work begins. “All the skilled among those doing the work made the tabernacle” (36:8). Curtains, boards, the veil; the ark, the table, the lampstand; two altars, a basin, the court, all the way down to the tent pegs (38:20).

They don’t make it in the order of the plan. On the mountain the ark came first and the tent afterwards. The makers begin with the tent, sensibly.

And this unit has one thing that no other unit in Exodus has. From its first word to its last, neither YHWH nor Elohim is named. The name is heard just before it begins, when the craftsmen speak of “the work that YHWH commanded” (36:5). It is heard again just after it ends, when we are told that Bezalel “made all that YHWH commanded Moses” (38:22). In between there is only “he made,” and “he made.”

The silence starts where the unit starts and stops where it stops.

The next unit announces itself. “These are the accounts of the tabernacle” (38:21).

First the metals are added up, and you can check the sum yourself. Every man who was counted paid half a shekel, and 603,550 men were counted. That is 301,775 shekels. The text says a hundred talents and 1,775 shekels (38:25). At three thousand shekels to the talent, it is the same figure. The hundred talents went into a hundred sockets, “a talent to a socket,” and the odd 1,775 into hooks and bands (38:27–28).

It balances to the shekel.

Then the account moves on from the metals to the yarn. “And from the blue and the purple and the scarlet they made garments of service … and they made the holy garments for Aaron, as YHWH commanded Moses” (39:1).

The yarn is the clothes. So the reckoning runs straight on into the making of them: the ephod, the breastpiece, the robe, the gold plate. And every one of them ends with those same words, “as YHWH commanded Moses.”

The accounts had begun with nearly the same words, in the line about Bezalel. Nothing like them is said in the giving, or in the making of the house. That is what makes the accounts and the clothes one unit: one reckoning of what was used, from the gold down to the yarn, and one refrain.

It is not what ends the unit, though. The refrain goes on. What ends it is the next sentence: “All the work of the tabernacle of the tent of meeting was finished” (39:32).

The making is over. From here on nothing is made. Things are brought to Moses, he is told where each one goes, and he puts it there.

“And Moses finished the work” (40:33).

“Finished” has now been said twice, and the last unit could stop. It has five verses more. The cloud covers the tent. The glory of YHWH fills the tabernacle. And Moses, who has just put it up with his own hands, can’t get in.

It may be that these are just neighbours that happen to share something. Or it may be that the units were set down two by two. Is Exodus nineteen pieces laid end to end, or is it something that has to be seen whole?

Part B: The Map of Exodus

Two Books in One

Exodus starts out as one kind of book and ends as another.

For the better part of twenty-four chapters it is a story, and a good one. A family goes down to Egypt and becomes a people. The people are made slaves, a baby is pulled out of the river, there are plagues, a sea opens, a mountain smokes. Then, at chapter 25, the story stops and a building begins: gold and linen, boards and sockets, fifty loops and fifty clasps. And when it has all been described down to the tent pegs, it is described again, this time as it is made. Readers have been known to skip.

A story and a blueprint. Why are they one book?

Before asking why, look at how each of the two is put together. Part A found three units that belong with nothing around them: the sea, the covenant at the foot of the mountain, and the tablets. See where they fall. The covenant comes just where the story ends and the building begins. The sea comes halfway through the story, with Egypt before it and the road after. The tablets come halfway through the building, with the plan before them and the work after. And each time, what lies on either side is four units.

A list at one end and the same list at the other. Then nine, and one. The four units in Egypt are two and two.

But first the three in the middle. Why isn’t the sea simply the last thing that happens in Egypt?

The Three in the Middle

By the time the people reach the sea, they have already left Egypt. Pharaoh told them to go, in the middle of the night, and they went. The unit that tells of that night is finished, too. It ends looking far ahead, with the son who will one day ask what it was all about, and a sign to wear on the hand (13:14–16).

But Egypt has not finished with them. Pharaoh changes his mind and comes after them with six hundred chariots, and they are caught with the water in front of them. Moses tells them to stand still: “the Egypt you see today you will never see again” (14:13). By morning it is so. “Israel saw Egypt dead on the shore of the sea” (14:30). Miriam picks up a drum, and the women dance.

So the sea is not the last thing that happens in Egypt. They are out of Egypt before it begins. And it is not the first thing that happens on the road. The road begins in the very next verse, and names its starting point: “Moses made Israel journey from the Sea of Reeds” (15:22). The leaving is over, and the journey has not started. The sea is what lies between.

The text marks the place from both sides. Just before the sea, the people are given the Passover: “This day shall be for you a remembrance, and you shall keep it as a feast to YHWH for your generations” (12:14). Just after it, on the road, Moses has a jar filled with manna: “to be kept for your generations, so that they may see the bread with which I fed you in the wilderness” (16:32). In the whole first half of the book, “for your generations” is said of these two things only. On each side of the water there is a special kind of bread, unleavened bread before it and bread from the sky after it, and each is to be remembered.

Now go to the other half of the book and look at the same spot. The plan is one long speech on the mountain, and the last thing in it is the Sabbath: “The children of Israel shall keep the Sabbath … for their generations, a covenant for ever” (31:16). Then the voice has “finished speaking,” and Moses is handed two tablets of stone (31:18). For three chapters after that nothing is planned and nothing is built. Something does get made, but it isn’t on the plan. There is the calf, and the tablets are broken at the foot of the mountain; Moses pleads, goes up again, and comes down with a second pair, “the two tablets of the testimony in Moses’ hand” (34:29). Then he gathers the people, and the building begins. The first thing he tells them about is the Sabbath (35:2).

These three units will each have a commentary of their own, like every other unit, and there is a good deal in them. On the map they have one job. They divide: the covenant divides the book, and the sea and the tablets divide its halves. The rest of this part is about what lies between them.

The Four Quarters

Start with a promise. In Egypt, before the first plague, YHWH gives Moses a sentence to take to the people: “I will take you to me for a people, and I will be your deity” (6:7). The people are too worn down by their labour to listen (6:9). It is a pity, because the sentence is the whole book.

It has two clauses, and Exodus has two halves. The story is how they are taken as a people. When they reach the mountain YHWH can say it is done: “I carried you on eagles’ wings and brought you to me” (19:4). The second half takes up the other clause, and the plan repeats it almost word for word: “I will dwell in the midst of the children of Israel, and I will be their deity” (29:45). The two parties to the covenant get a half of the book each.

Each half has two quarters. The second half is the easy one, because it tells the same thing twice. The first time, Moses hears it alone on the mountain: “According to all that I show you, the pattern of the tabernacle and the pattern of all its vessels, so you shall make it” (25:9). The second time, everybody is doing it. Moses gathers “all the congregation of the children of Israel” (35:1), they bring what is needed, and piece after piece is made “as YHWH commanded Moses.”

The story goes the same way, and it says so in advance. At the bush Moses is given a sign: “When you bring the people out of Egypt, you shall serve the deity on this mountain” (3:12). It is an odd sign, since nobody can see it until everything is over. But it lays the story out in two steps: first out of Egypt, then to this mountain. In Egypt one man is told what is going to happen, and the people he is sent to don’t listen. At the mountain “all the people answered together and said, All that YHWH has spoken we will do” (19:8).

In Egypt they build store cities for Pharaoh (1:11). They make bricks, and when Moses asks for a few days off, the straw is taken away and the number of bricks stays the same. The First Quarter: Egypt

The four units set in Egypt tell one story, from a family’s arrival to the night a people walks out.

It begins with a people in trouble and one man. The family that came down to Egypt has grown until a new king is afraid of it. He sets it to hard labour and orders its sons into the Nile. One of those sons goes into the river in a basket, is drawn out by the king’s own daughter, and grows up in the palace. Then one day he sees an Egyptian striking a Hebrew. He looks this way and that, strikes the Egyptian, and hides him in the sand. Pharaoh hears of it and wants him dead, and Moses runs. He is keeping sheep in the wilderness when he comes on a bush that burns and is not burnt up. He is told that the ground under him is holy, and he is told, at length and against his protests, what is going to happen and what he is to do about it.

So he goes back. He and his brother Aaron gather the elders, the people believe them, and the two go in to Pharaoh. Pharaoh says no, and makes the work harder. The people stop listening.

Then come the plagues. Nine times something is taken from Egypt, and nine times the answer is the same.

And then the night. Every household of Israel takes a lamb, marks its door with the blood, and stays in. At midnight Egypt’s firstborn are struck, from Pharaoh’s house to the dungeon, and before morning Pharaoh is telling Israel to go and all Egypt is hurrying them out.

The Second Quarter: The Road and the Mountain

The second quarter begins three days past the sea, with nothing to drink, and it ends with a calendar. So did the first. The night of the going out opens with one: “This month shall be for you the head of the months; it is the first of the months of the year for you” (12:2). Keep an eye on the last unit of each quarter. The year turns up there every time.

It begins on the road, and the road has three stops. At the first the water can’t be drunk. The people grumble, a piece of wood sweetens it, and “there he set for them a statute and a judgment, and there he tested them” (15:25). At the second there is no bread. The whole congregation grumbles, and bread falls from the sky, with a double share on the sixth day and none on the seventh. At the third there is no water at all, and it comes out of a rock while the elders of Israel look on. Then Amalek attacks, and for the first time the people do something besides grumble. Joshua picks men and goes out to fight.

Then the mountain. A visitor arrives first. Jethro, Moses’ father-in-law, finds him sitting from morning to evening while the people stand in line, and asks what he thinks he is doing. Moses explains: “When they have a matter, it comes to me, and I judge between a man and his neighbour” (18:16). Jethro tells him he will wear himself out, and that he needs judges. So judges are appointed. Then the mountain is fenced, the whole people stands at the foot of it, and the Ten Words are spoken aloud to all of them. They can’t bear it, and they ask Moses to do the listening for them.

After that there is no more story. The voice goes on, case by case: a slave, a blow, an ox that gores, a pit left open, a thing left in a neighbour’s keeping.

And then the last unit, which is still law but has changed its subject. Now it is the stranger, the widow and the poor; the judges and the leader; the witnesses in a court; and at the end the year itself, a seventh year, a seventh day and three feasts.

The Third Quarter: The Plan

The third quarter has no story in it. Moses is up the mountain, inside the cloud, and for seven chapters one voice speaks and nobody answers. What it describes is a house, and it goes about it in a particular order.

It begins with the house itself. First a request: every man whose heart moves him is to bring gold, silver and bronze, blue and purple and scarlet yarn, and “let them make me a sanctuary, and I will dwell in their midst” (25:8). Then the things to be made, from the inside out: the ark with its cover and its two cherubim, a table, a lampstand, the tent with its curtains and boards, a veil to divide it, an altar, and a court around the whole. Moses isn’t given reasons. He is given measurements, and he is told more than once to make it the way it is being shown to him on the mountain.

Then the men who will go in. Aaron, Moses’ brother, is to be brought near with his four sons, and the first thing that happens to him is that he is dressed. The clothes are described as carefully as the house was: an ephod with a stone on each shoulder, a breastpiece with twelve stones, a robe hung with bells, a gold plate for his forehead.

Then the dressed man is made a priest. It takes seven days, a bull, two rams, oil and blood, and at the end of it the priests are holy and so is the altar.

And then the last unit, which is what goes on in the house once it is standing and the priests are in it. A lamb every morning and a lamb every evening. Incense, morning and evening, on a small altar that is purged “once in the year” (30:10). There is the year again, in the last unit. Half a shekel from every man who is counted. Water for washing, oil for anointing. Two craftsmen are named to make it all, and the last thing spoken of is the Sabbath.

The Fourth Quarter: The Building

The fourth quarter is the third over again with the verbs changed. “You shall make” becomes “he made,” and a good deal of it comes back word for word. But it isn’t arranged the way the plan was, and it has two things the plan could not have: a crowd at the beginning, and a cloud at the end.

It begins with the crowd. Moses gathers the whole congregation and tells them first about the Sabbath. Then he asks for what the plan asked for, gold, silver and bronze, yarn and skins, wood, oil and stones, from “everyone whose heart is willing” (35:5), and he tells them what is to be made of it, from the tabernacle and its tent down to the priests’ clothes. They go away, and they come back carrying things. Men and women bring brooches, earrings and rings. Women spin yarn and goats’ hair. The leaders bring the stones. Two craftsmen, Bezalel and Oholiab, are named and put in charge. And the gifts keep coming, “morning by morning” (36:3), until the craftsmen leave their work to tell Moses there is too much.

Then the house is made: curtains, boards and bars, the veil; the ark, the table and the lampstand; the two altars; a basin cast from the mirrors of the women who served at the door of the tent (38:8); and the court around it all. “He made” stands at the head of piece after piece.

Then the making stops for the accounts. “These are the accounts of the tabernacle” (38:21): so much gold, so much silver, so much bronze, and what was done with each. The silver is the half-shekels of the count, one from each of 603,550 men. When the metals have been accounted for, the priests’ clothes are made: the ephod with its two stones, the breastpiece, the robe with its bells, the gold plate.

And then the end. Everything is brought to Moses, and he looks it over. “Moses saw all the work, and behold, they had done it; as YHWH had commanded, so they had done. And Moses blessed them” (39:43). On the first day of the first month he sets it up, piece by piece. He puts the testimony into the ark and the ark behind its veil, lays out the bread, lights the lamps and burns the incense. “And Moses finished the work” (40:33). The cloud covers the tent, and the glory of YHWH fills the tabernacle.

The Four Corners

The last section stopped with Moses outside the tent. That is the last unit on the map, at its bottom right corner. Now go to the other three corners.

The first corner is the bush. Moses turns aside to look, and a voice stops him: “Do not come near. Take your sandals off your feet, for the place on which you stand is holy ground” (3:5). One man, a patch of ground, and a line he may not cross.

At the far end of the top row is the mountain. This time the whole people is there, and the line is drawn around all of them: “You shall set bounds for the people all around … whoever touches the mountain shall surely be put to death” (19:12). A little later Moses says it back to YHWH: “You warned us, saying, Set bounds around the mountain and make it holy” (19:23).

At the foot of the first column is the altar. For seven days Aaron and his sons are made holy at it, and the food of those days is theirs alone: “an outsider shall not eat, for they are holy” (29:33). Then the altar itself: “the altar shall be most holy; whoever touches the altar shall become holy” (29:37).

And at the last corner Moses is told to anoint the tabernacle and everything in it, “and it shall be holy” (40:9). He sets it up, the cloud covers it, and he cannot come in.

The plan is different, because the whole plan is holy ground. A veil is hung to divide “the holy place from the most holy” (26:33). The priests are warned more than once what to do “so that they do not die” (28:35; 30:20). And of the vessels, once they are anointed, it is said just as it is of the altar: “whoever touches them shall become holy” (30:29). So the altar does not stand out in its quarter the way the bush stands out in Egypt. What it has is its corner, and its words.

The words are worth a second look. At the mountain, kol ha-noge’a, “whoever touches,” is put to death. At the altar, kol ha-noge’a becomes holy. The phrase is used in Exodus of the mountain, the altar and the anointed vessels, and of nothing else. In the story, to touch the holy place is to die. In the tabernacle, to touch it is to be made holy.

And the other two corners have the same man at each. At the bush Moses is told not to come near. At the tabernacle he cannot come in. The book opens and closes with Moses stopped at a line.

Corners have mattered before. On the map of Genesis, the four corners of the stories of the fathers all hold one scene: a woman of the family in a foreign ruler’s house, and a contact that is forbidden. Sarah is taken into Pharaoh’s house, and YHWH strikes Pharaoh for it; the Hebrew says he “touched” him with great “touches” (Gen 12:17). She is taken again by Abimelech, who “had not come near her,” and who is told in a dream, “I did not let you touch her” (Gen 20:4, 6). Rebekah is passed off as a sister, and when Abimelech finds out he gives an order to all his people: “Whoever touches this man or his wife shall surely be put to death” (Gen 26:11). At the fourth corner nobody steps in, and it is the worst of the four. Dinah is taken by Shechem, the son of the prince of the land. The text says, “such a thing is not done” (Gen 34:7), and then tells what it cost: her brothers “came upon the city … and killed every male” (Gen 34:25).

Now listen again to the two top corners of Exodus. “Do not come near.” “Whoever touches the mountain shall surely be put to death.” The first is what Abimelech had not done to Sarah. The second is Abimelech’s order about Rebekah, nearly word for word, with a mountain where the man and his wife were.

In Genesis the corners are where someone must not be touched, and the one time it happens a city dies. In Exodus they are where a holy place must not be touched.

In both books the corners are the edge of the map. And in both, what stands at them is about an edge: a line that is not to be crossed.

There is one more set of corners, and they are small ones. The four we have just seen are places. These four are times. I asked you to keep an eye on the last unit of each quarter. Here they are together.

Where the Pattern Is Thin

A map like this rests on many small things pointing the same way, and they are not all equally strong. Here is where I would press if I were reading it for the first time.

The firmest parts are the plainest. The book divides four, one, four, twice over, and nobody has to be persuaded of that. Egypt falls into two pairs by its two lists of names and its nine plagues and one. In every quarter the first pair is told about the people and the second about what is YHWH’s. And at each corner of the map there is a holy place with a line around it.

The pairs are not all held as tightly as Egypt’s. A list repeated and a count of ten split nine and one are hard to argue with. The road and the mountain share one word, “test.” The two collections of law share a heading and a six and a seventh. The house and the priests’ clothes share their materials. Those are real, but they are lighter, and a reader may fairly take the clothes as simply the next thing on the list after the house.

Reading across the pairs is where the quarters differ most, and I have not hidden it. In Egypt it is a few and then all the people, and it is clear. In the plan it is the same, but faint. On the road and at the mountain it is something else, what a people needs to live and then what makes it holy. In the building it is each one’s own and then one thing for all. These are kin to one another, but they are not one thing said four times. The loosest is the building’s second pair. The accounts and the priests’ clothes are two different things in one unit, and neither becomes the standing tabernacle as plainly as the gifts become the house.

The altar is the weakest of the four corners inside its own quarter, for the reason given: the whole plan is holy ground.

And two smaller things. “Touch” is an ordinary word, in Genesis and in Exodus. What carries the comparison is a whole sentence, “whoever touches … shall surely be put to death,” said by a king of Gerar about a man and his wife and by YHWH about a mountain. And the year is at the end of every quarter, but the feasts are also listed in the unit of the tablets, which is no quarter’s end.

None of this unmakes the map. It tells you which parts of it will bear weight.

What Comes Next

We have read the map one quarter at a time. But once four quarters are set in a square, they also make long lines. The bush, the first plagues, the house and the priests made holy stand in one column, the first unit of each pair down the whole length of the map. The bush, the refusing before Pharaoh, the road and the mountain stand in one row, which runs from one side of the book to the other and crosses the sea on the way. And the sea, the covenant and the tablets make a column of their own down the middle.

Part C reads the map that way, by its columns and its rows, and asks what the units in each line have to do with one another.

Part C: Columns and Rows

One Man, and All the House of Israel

Part B ended with two scenes. In the first, a man keeping sheep in the wilderness turns aside to look at a bush on fire, and a voice tells him not to come near. In the last, a cloud lies on the tabernacle by day and there is fire in it by night, “in the sight of all the house of Israel” (40:38).

One man saw the first fire. Everybody sees the last.

How does the book get from the one to the other? By telling its story, of course. But the map gives us another way to look. In Part B we read it a quarter at a time. Now go back to the bush, and instead of reading on, read straight down.

Under the bush are the nine plagues. They are loud, and all Egypt is in them. But look at who takes part. YHWH speaks to Moses. Moses and Aaron stand in front of Pharaoh. And the people of Israel? They are in Goshen, where the hail doesn’t fall (9:26). They are talked about in every scene, and they do nothing in any of them.

Keep going down, into the second half of the book. Moses is on the mountain, inside the cloud, and the people are at the bottom. He is being shown a house, piece by piece: “According to all that I show you” (25:9).

And under the house are seven days in which Moses washes his brother, dresses him and anoints him. “This is the thing that you shall do to them, to make them holy, to be priests to me” (29:1).

Four units, a long way apart in the book. In each of them YHWH is with Moses, and the people are somewhere else: back in Egypt, off in Goshen, at the foot of the mountain, outside the door.

Now go to the other side of the map and read down again.

At the top is the mountain, and this time nobody is missing. “Israel camped there, facing the mountain” (19:2). YHWH comes down “in the sight of all the people” (19:11).

Under the mountain are the last laws. There is no story in them, but there is a “you” on every line, and it is a whole people’s life that is being talked about: its strangers and widows, its judges, its fields, its feasts.

Under the laws the house is made, by “all the skilled among those doing the work” (36:8).

And at the bottom, “the children of Israel did all the work” (39:42). The tabernacle goes up, and the cloud covers it in front of everyone.

The First Column and the Last

Put the two outside columns next to each other, and this time go across.

Start at the top. At the bush Moses is given a sign that he has no way of checking: “When you bring the people out of Egypt, you shall serve the deity on this mountain” (3:12). At the top of the last column they are there. It is the same mountain, and there is fire on it again: “YHWH came down on it in fire” (19:18).

What was promised to one man at the top of the first column has happened to everyone at the top of the last.

The next pair across looks less likely. One is the plagues, and the other is a list of laws.

But watch what happens to Egypt, a step at a time. It starts in Pharaoh’s court, with a staff that turns into a serpent. Nobody is hurt. Then the river stinks. Then the herds die. Then the crops are gone and the land is dark, and Pharaoh’s own servants say to him, “Do you not yet know that Egypt is lost?” (10:7).

A kingdom is coming apart, a step at a time. And the text says why, to Pharaoh’s face: “so that you may know that I am YHWH in the midst of the land” (8:18 [English 8:22]). Egypt is the country where he is not known.

The last laws go the other way. They begin with three who are to have no place in it at all: the sorceress, the man who lies with a beast, and the one who sacrifices to other deities (22:17–19 [English 22:18–20]). Next come the weak, who are to be given a place: the stranger, the widow, the poor man. Then the judges and the leader. Then a court, with its witnesses. And at the end a whole nation keeping one year, with a seventh day and three feasts.

A step at a time, a country is being put together. Paul Hocking and I, writing about these laws, called it “nation-building.”² And YHWH is in it. From the widow’s cry to the feasts, he says “I” and “me.”

In the first column a state is taken apart, where YHWH is not known. In the last a nation is put together, with YHWH in the middle of it.

Go down again, to the house. It is shown to Moses in pieces: an ark, a table, a lampstand, curtains, boards. When the curtains are joined, the plan says what the joining is for: “and the tabernacle shall be one” (26:6). Across the map the curtains are sewn and the clasps go in, “and the tabernacle was one” (36:13).

So the first column has the pieces, and the last has the whole.

And at the foot, the seven days. One family is washed, dressed, anointed and made holy. Across the map the same orders are given again (40:12–15), on the day the tabernacle goes up. Only now a whole house is anointed around them, and the glory fills it.

What about everyone else? That was said at the head of the last column, before anything was built: “You shall be to me a kingdom of priests and a holy nation” (19:6). The first column has one family of priests. The last has a nation of them.

One more thing belongs with these two columns. Part B found a holy place with a line around it at each corner of the map: the ground at the bush, the mountain, the altar, the tabernacle. The corners are the tops and the feet of these two columns. Lines are drawn in other places too. The priests must wash “so that they do not die” (30:20), and when Moses goes up for the second tablets no one else may be seen on the mountain (34:3). But the four that Part B found stand at the four ends of this pair.

So the two outside columns are two ends. The question is how the book gets from one to the other, and for that we go inside.

The Second Column

In the first column the people were always somewhere else. Take one step in from the bush and they begin to arrive.

They arrive first as a word in a message. Moses is on his way back to Egypt, and he has been told what to say to Pharaoh: “Israel is my son, my firstborn. Send my son … If you refuse, I will kill your son, your firstborn” (4:22–23).

So the people have a name now. And it comes with a price: a son for a son.

On the way, at a lodging place, YHWH “met him and sought to kill him.” Moses’ wife circumcises their son, and it stops (4:24–26). The text doesn’t say why, or even which of them was in danger. But hold on to the scene: a son circumcised, blood, and a death that passes by. It is going to happen again, to everybody.

Pharaoh refuses, of course. And before the unit ends, the story stops for a list: “the heads of their fathers’ houses” (6:14). The people who were called one son are set out house by house.

House by house is how it goes on. On the night of the going out, each house takes a lamb and puts the blood on its own door (12:3, 7). At midnight Egypt’s firstborn die. And at every door in Israel there is blood, and the death passes by.

The circumcision is there too. The night has a rule about who may eat the lamb: “no uncircumcised man shall eat of it” (12:48). What a mother did with a flint at the lodging place is now the law in every house.

And then the firstborn are claimed. “Sanctify to me every firstborn … it is mine” (13:2). On the road, “my firstborn” was a way of speaking about Israel. Now every house has one.

A people, and then each of its houses. That is the upper half of the column. The lower half picks up from there.

If they are his, they have to come before him. They don’t all come at once. One family is brought forward “from among the children of Israel” (28:1), and one man is dressed to go in. Look at what he is wearing. The names of the sons of Israel are on his shoulders, and again over his heart, a stone to a tribe (28:12, 21).

The list is back, and Aaron has it on. When he goes in, they go in with him.

One step is left: from a name that someone else carries to the man himself. At the foot of the column every man of twenty and over is counted, and each pays half a shekel, “a ransom for his life … so that there be no plague among them” (30:12).

We have heard that before. It was said of the blood on the door (12:13).

The Fourth Column

Step over the middle column for now, and begin again at the top, on the road.

The people are out of Egypt, and for the first time they have something to divide among themselves. There is bread on the ground every morning, and a rule for gathering it: “an omer a head” (16:16). Some gather a lot and some a little. Then they measure, “and he who gathered much had nothing over, and he who gathered little had no lack” (16:18).

They have worked to a daily count before. “Each day’s share in its day,” the taskmasters said, and it was bricks (5:13). On the road the phrase comes back, word for word (16:4). This time it is bread, and everyone gets the same.

Even shares are easy when the bread falls from the sky. The next unit down is about when it isn’t easy. The cases are all about “a man” and “his neighbour.” A man’s ox gores his neighbour’s ox. A man leaves a pit open. A man loses what his neighbour gave him to keep. And case after case ends on one verb: he shall pay. The Hebrew is shallem, to make whole.

So far they share alike, and they make good what they break. Below the covenant they go a step further. They give. Nobody measures this time. “Every man and woman whose heart moved them” (35:29) brings a ring, a skin, a length of yarn, all to one piece of work. They have to be told to stop.

And at the foot of the column is the count. Remember the half shekel, the ransom each man paid at the foot of the second column? Here is what became of it. “A beka a head,” from 603,550 men (38:26), came to a hundred talents of silver and a little over. The hundred talents were cast into a hundred sockets, “a talent to a socket” (38:27).

Those are the feet the walls of the tabernacle stand in. The ransom silver is under the wall.

The Middle Column

That leaves the column we stepped over: the sea, the covenant and the tablets.

Think of what stands on either side of it now. To the left are one man, and the houses claimed one by one. To the right are those same people joined together, and then the whole nation. In the middle you would expect to find both at once. You do, in all three units.

At the sea, when it is over, “they believed in YHWH and in Moses his servant” (14:31). Then they sing, “Moses and the children of Israel” together. And what the whole people sings is “I”: “I will sing to YHWH” (15:1).

At the covenant the two are set side by side. “Moses alone shall come near” (24:2). In the next verse, “all the people answered with one voice” (24:3). One man goes up, and one voice answers from below.

At the tablets it nearly comes apart. YHWH offers to begin again with Moses by himself: “I will make of you a great nation” (32:10). That would be the first column without any of the others. Moses won’t have it. Whenever he pleads, it is for the two together: “I and your people” (33:16). And that is how the unit ends: “I have made a covenant with you and with Israel” (34:27).

The Four Rows

We have read down each column and set the columns side by side. Now do the same with the rows. Read along each one, and set them one above another, from the top of the map to the bottom. A row has four units. The sea, the covenant and the tablets lie between the rows and belong to none.

A word of warning first. Going down the rows is not going forward in time, any more than going across the columns was. Every row has two units from before the sea or the tablets and two from after. This is the order in which the book has set things out, and not the order in which they happen.

Begin with a question the people ask in the top row. They are at Rephidim with nothing to drink, and they say, “Is YHWH in our midst or not?” (17:7). Keep it in mind on the way down.

The top row is the bush, the refusing before Pharaoh, the road and the mountain. Where is YHWH to be met in it? The row keeps coming back to one mountain. Moses finds him there at the start (3:1), and is told to bring the people back to the same place (3:12). Aaron meets Moses there (4:27). The water on the road comes out of a rock “at Horeb” (17:6). And at the end of the row everyone is camped at its foot. In the row beneath, the word “mountain” is not found once.

How do they know he is there? By wonders. A bush burns and isn’t burnt up. A staff turns into a serpent. Bread falls from the sky. The mountain is on fire. And nobody may come close: “whoever touches the mountain shall surely be put to death” (19:12).

Go down a row, to the plagues, the night, the cases and the last laws. Here he is met in a country. “I am YHWH in the midst of the land” (8:18 [English 8:22]). On the night of the going out he is nearer than that, at the doors of the houses: “YHWH will pass over the door” (12:23).

These are still wonders, the greatest in the book. But something new comes with them. The night is to be kept, year after year, as “a holy assembly” (12:16). The firstborn who were spared are to be made holy (13:2). The wonder has been given a holy day.

And then, in the same row, the wonders stop. In the laws nothing out of the ordinary happens at all. But YHWH has not gone away. He is closer than before, inside the nation’s daily life, and he is speaking: “If he cries out to me, I will surely hear” (22:22 [English 22:23]). What he asks for is this: “holy people you shall be to me” (22:30 [English 22:31]).

So in the second row he is in the land, and then in the nation. The wonders are giving way to something else.

The third row is the house, the clothes, the giving and the making. There isn’t a wonder in it. The craftsmen are filled with “the spirit of Elohim, in wisdom, in understanding and in knowledge” (35:31), and what they do with it is spin, cut, cast and sew. What the row is about is a place: “Let them make me a sanctuary, and I will dwell in their midst” (25:8). The Hebrew for sanctuary, mikdash, is the word “holy” made into a place. The clothes are “holy garments” (28:2). The work is “the holy work” (36:4). The people are making a holy place with their hands.

And in the bottom row he is in it. The priests and the altar are made holy, with oil and blood and water. The gold plate for Aaron’s forehead is made, and the words are cut into it: “Holy to YHWH” (39:30). And the question from Rephidim gets its answer: “I will dwell in the midst of the children of Israel” (29:45). At the very end the cloud covers the tent and the glory fills it. It is the one wonder in the two lower rows, and it happens at the house.

But on the night the sign is something the people do: “the blood shall be a sign for you on the houses” (12:13). Part B said that one sentence holds the whole book, and that the book gives it twice. “I will take you to me for a people, and I will be your deity” (6:7). “I will dwell in the midst of the children of Israel, and I will be their deity” (29:45).

Where the Pattern Is Thin

As in Part B, here is where I would press.

The firmest thing is the first column against the last. Every unit of one has Moses with YHWH and the people elsewhere. Every unit of the other has the people present and at work. The four holy places of Part B stand at their four ends, though they are not the only lines the book draws (30:20; 34:3). There are two cautions. The first unit of the book begins with the whole people, and only narrows to one man. And the unit in which the house is made is the one unit of Exodus that names neither YHWH nor Elohim. It is in the last column because of who does the work.

The plagues and the last laws share almost no words. What they share is a movement, a step at a time, in opposite directions: one takes a country apart and the other puts one together. A reader who doesn’t grant that movement will not see the pair.

In the tabernacle half the columns mostly answer each other as plan and carrying out, and that would be so however the map were drawn. What the map adds is which unit carries out which, and there are two loose ends. The request for gifts that opens the house (25:1–9) is carried out in the giving, in the fourth column. The incense altar and the basin of the daily service (30:1–10, 17–21) are made along with the house, in the last (37:25–29; 38:8).

The second column rests a good deal on one reading: that the lodging place and the night of the going out are two versions of one scene. They share a son, a circumcision, blood and a death that passes by. But the text does not connect them in so many words.

“Claimed” and “joined” are my words for the second and fourth columns, and there are things on the wrong side of each. The line between Israel and Egypt is drawn most plainly in the plagues (8:18 [English 8:22]; 9:4; 11:7), which are in the first column. “I will dwell in the midst of the children of Israel” (29:45) is a joining, and it stands in the second. And the road, for all its shared bread, has a quarrel and a war on it.

In the middle column, Moses and the whole people are in one scene at the mountain as well. What the three middle units have that the mountain does not is the two named side by side, and someone going before the people.

The rows are a matter of weight, not a clean cut. There is a good deal that is holy in the top row: the ground at the bush, the Sabbath of the manna (16:23), the people made ready at the mountain (19:10). And the cloud at the bottom is a wonder if anything is. The cases say very little of YHWH (21:13–14; 22:10 [English 22:11]). And the plan of the house, in the third row, is his own speech from the mountain. It belongs to that row by what it is about, a place for the people to make.

What Comes Next

We have now read the map three ways: a quarter at a time, by its columns, and by its rows. The same nineteen units were in front of us each time, and nothing was moved.

One thing is left before the units are taken one at a time. Exodus stops, but it doesn’t come to an end. On its last page the people are still camped at the mountain, and the tabernacle has been set up, with its priests not yet made priests. Part D follows the map out of the book, to see where its two halves go, and what lies beneath its columns in Leviticus.

Part D: Architecture and Meaning in Exodus

Where the Two Halves Go

Part B began with a question. Exodus is a story for twenty-four chapters and a building for sixteen. A story and a blueprint. Why are they one book? Parts B and C answered from inside the book: a people, a house for YHWH, and a covenant between the two. There is more to say, and it starts where the book stops.

Look at how Exodus ends. Both of its halves lead out of it.

Take the story first. The people reach the mountain “in the third month” after leaving Egypt, and “Israel camped there, facing the mountain” (19:1–2). And there they stay. Laws are given, a covenant is made, a calf is made, a tabernacle is built, and nobody strikes a tent. The last verse of the book speaks of “all their journeys” (40:38), but they haven’t gone anywhere. To see them leave you have to go two books on: “In the second year, in the second month, on the twentieth of the month, the cloud lifted from over the tabernacle of the testimony, and the children of Israel set out on their journeys from the wilderness of Sinai” (Numbers 10:11–12).

Now the building. It is standing, the glory has filled it, and Moses can’t get in (40:35). That is where Exodus leaves him. Turn the page: “He called to Moses, and YHWH spoke to him from the tent of meeting” (Leviticus 1:1).

The voice is inside now, and Leviticus begins there, with what is done in the tabernacle: offerings at the altar, the priests made priests, and once a year one man going behind the veil, to the ark itself (Leviticus 16:2). Before the book ends it has moved out to the people who live with that tent in the middle of them, their houses, their courts and their feasts.

When Leviticus is over the tabernacle is still the subject, but something about it has changed. At the beginning of Numbers it is a thing that has to travel, and it is given to the Levites: “When the tabernacle sets out, the Levites shall take it down, and when the tabernacle camps, the Levites shall set it up” (Numbers 1:51).

They were not the ones who built it. In Exodus the tabernacle is made by the skilled among the people, and Moses puts it up. The Levites are there only in the accounts of the metals, in a phrase: “the service of the Levites, by the hand of Ithamar” (38:21). The tabernacle becomes theirs when it has to be moved.

So the tabernacle is put together at the end of Exodus, served all through Leviticus, and made ready to be taken apart and carried at the beginning of Numbers. Only then does the cloud lift, and the story that stopped at the mountain starts again.

There are three things here, one inside another. The story is on the outside, at both ends. Inside it is the tabernacle as a thing that is built and moved. And inside that is what is done in it, all the way in to the place behind the veil.

We have seen this picture before, or rather the people in the books have. It is how they camp. “The children of Israel shall camp each by his own flag … at a distance, around the tent of meeting” (Numbers 2:2). Inside them, “the Levites shall camp around the tabernacle of the testimony” (Numbers 1:53). And at the door, on the east, “Moses and Aaron and his sons, keeping the charge of the sanctuary” (Numbers 3:38).

So here is something to add to the answer. Exodus is a story and a building in one book because it is where two things begin. Its first half opens the story, which is all Israel’s and runs around the outside. Its second half opens the tabernacle, which stands in the middle. And between its two halves is the covenant.

“Let them make me a sanctuary, and I will dwell in their midst” (25:8). In their midst is where the books put it.

Straight Down

If the tabernacle goes straight on from Exodus into Leviticus, it is fair to ask whether the map does. In Part C we read Exodus by its columns. Does a column stop at the bottom of the book?

Start with a loose end. At the foot of the first column are the seven days in which Aaron and his sons are to be made priests. It is all command: “seven days you shall fill their hand” (29:35). On the day the tabernacle goes up Moses is told again to wash them, dress them and anoint them (40:12–15). The setting up is then told piece by piece. The seven days are not. Exodus ends without them.

They are in Leviticus. “Moses brought Aaron and his sons near, and washed them with water” (Leviticus 8:6). He dresses them and anoints them, and then: “You shall not go out from the door of the tent of meeting for seven days … for seven days he shall fill your hand” (8:33). “And Aaron and his sons did all the things that YHWH commanded by the hand of Moses” (8:36).

So the foot of the first column is an order, and the carrying out lies under it, in the next book.

To see whether that is more than one loose end, we need a picture of Leviticus. The commentary on Leviticus draws it properly. Here is the short version.

Leviticus has a middle that stands alone, as Exodus has. It is chapter 19, which begins “You shall be holy.”

Around that chapter the book is built in pairs: a group of chapters before it, answered by a group after it. Start at the middle and work outward.

The pair nearest the middle is chapters 16 to 18 and chapters 20 to 22:25. Both keep listing a person’s relatives.

The next pair out is chapters 8 to 12 and chapters 22:26 to 24. Both keep counting “seven days,” and then “the eighth day” (8:33 and 9:1; 12:2–3; 22:27; 23:36).

The outermost pair is chapters 1 to 7 and chapters 25 to 27. Both say where the laws were given: at the tent of meeting, and at Mount Sinai (1:1; 7:38; 25:1; 27:34).

That leaves chapters 13 to 15, the laws of impurity, which have no partner. The commentary on Leviticus reads them as a curtain hung in front of the innermost pair, and sets them to one side.

Three pairs, then, around a middle. Exodus has two pairs of columns around its own middle. Lay Leviticus under Exodus, middle to middle, and its two inner pairs fall under the four columns. Its outermost pair sticks out at both ends, with nothing above it.

None of this was arranged for the sake of Exodus. The groups of Leviticus, their pairing and the setting aside of chapters 13 to 15 all come from Leviticus read by itself. What is new here is only laying one book over the other, middle to middle.

Now read down each column and keep going.

The first column is how things stand at the start: one man at the bush, the pieces of a house, a family not yet made priests. Under it we have already found the one thing that is certain. The seven days ordered at its foot are carried out in the first chapter beneath it.

The rest of the group has echoes of the column, and they are lighter. Go on from the seven days and see what comes next.

On the eighth day the new priests make their first offerings. Fire comes out and takes what is on the altar, “and the glory of YHWH appeared to all the people” (9:23–24).

Then two of Aaron’s sons bring fire of their own, which nobody had asked for. Fire comes out a second time, and this time it takes them (10:2). Moses tells their father what YHWH had said: “Through those near to me I will be made holy” (10:3).

Now go back to the top of the column, to the first thing the voice says at the bush. “Do not come near” (3:5). One man heard it and took off his sandals. Here two men come near, and die of it.

After that the group turns to animals. Israel is to tell apart the ones it may eat from the ones it may not (11:47), and the reason it is given is Egypt: “I am YHWH who brings you up from the land of Egypt” (11:45). In the plagues the telling apart was done for them. The swarms kept out of Goshen (8:18 [English 8:22]), and before the last plague it is said outright: “YHWH sets Egypt apart from Israel” (11:7). (The Hebrew verb is a different one.)

And the group ends with a birth. “When a woman conceives and bears a male” (12:2). That is how Exodus begins, with two midwives and a woman who “conceived and bore a son” (2:2).

First offerings, a first lesson in telling one thing from another, a birth. The first column is how things stand at the start. So is this.

The second column was about how the people are claimed for YHWH: house by house, with blood on the door and a death that passes by, and at its foot half a shekel from every man, “to atone for your lives” (30:15).

Under it is the day on which Aaron goes behind the veil. He goes in with blood, and he is told how to do it “so that he does not die” (16:2). He atones “for himself and for his house” (16:6), and it is said three times before the whole assembly is added (16:11, 17). The day comes “once in the year” (16:34). Exodus has that phrase in one place, at the foot of this column (30:10). Leviticus has it only here.

The next chapter turns from Aaron’s house to everyone’s. “Any man of the house of Israel” who slaughters an ox, a sheep or a goat must bring it to the door of the tent of meeting, where the priest throws its blood against the altar (17:3–6). On the night of the going out the blood went on the door of his own house. And the chapter says what the blood is for: “I have given it to you on the altar, to atone for your lives” (17:11). That is the half shekel’s phrase. In Exodus it is said only of the silver, and in Leviticus only of the blood.

The last chapter of the three begins, “You shall not do as they do in the land of Egypt, where you lived” (18:3).

The middle column had the one man and the whole people together, and at its centre the covenant, where “all the people answered with one voice” (24:3).

Under the covenant is chapter 19. It is spoken “to all the congregation of the children of Israel” (19:2), and what it asks, it asks of each of them. Its laws are set out in two columns, as the commentary on Leviticus shows: what a person owes by himself, and what he owes among other people.

The chapter also remembers the calf. Among the words given with the second tablets is this: “Molten deities you shall not make for yourself” (34:17). Among the first commands of chapter 19 is this: “Molten deities you shall not make for yourselves” (19:4). Neither book has the phrase anywhere else.

The fourth column was about how the people belong to one another. In the middle of it are the cases: a man breaks something, and he makes it good.

Under the column, Leviticus goes through cases of its own, and this time it says who has to see to them. A man gives his child to Molech, and “the people of the land shall stone him” (20:2). One law is the same in both books: whoever curses his father or his mother shall be put to death (Exodus 21:17; Leviticus 20:9).

Lower in the column, every man and woman brought “a freewill gift to YHWH” for the tabernacle (35:29). Under it are the rules for a freewill gift (22:18, 21).

The last column had YHWH present with the whole people: the mountain, a nation’s laws, the house made, the glory.

The group under it begins with the nation’s year, feast by feast (chapter 23). Above it are the last laws, with their “three times in the year” (23:14).

Then come a lamp and a table. They were made when the house was made (37:10, 17). On the day the tabernacle went up, Moses laid bread on the table and lit the lamps (40:23, 25). That was once. Here it is “continually”: the lamps every evening, the bread every Sabbath (24:2–8).

Then a man curses the Name, and “all the congregation shall stone him” (24:14). At the head of the column the whole people stood at the foot of the mountain and were warned that whoever touched it would be stoned (19:13).

The group ends, “and the children of Israel did as YHWH commanded Moses” (24:23). The same words are heard near the end of Exodus, when the finished work is brought to Moses: “as YHWH commanded Moses, so the children of Israel did” (39:42).

These are not all of one strength. The seven days are plain: an order in one book, carried out in the next. In the second column and in the middle, each book has a phrase in one place only, and the place in Leviticus is directly under the place in Exodus. The other three columns have less.

Still, it is hard to think that the map of Exodus was drawn to stop at the bottom of the book.

The Same Turn

Step back from the columns and look at the two books whole.

Part C read Exodus across, from one man at the bush to “all the house of Israel,” with the covenant in the middle. Leviticus goes the same way. Before chapter 19 its laws are mostly about one person at a time: someone who brings an offering, a priest, a woman who has given birth, the one man who goes behind the veil. After chapter 19 they are about the community: its courts, its feasts, its land. The commentary on Leviticus follows that through.

Set one book over the other and the turn comes at the same place. In Exodus the one and the many meet in a covenant. Directly under it, all the congregation is told, “You shall be holy, for I YHWH your deity am holy.”

The other reading of Part C goes on as well. Down the rows of Exodus, YHWH came from the mountain into a house in the midst of the people, and the wonders turned into holy days and holy things. At the bottom of the map the cloud was on the tent and Moses was outside it. At the opening of the next book the voice calls him from within.

And the sentence goes on with it. Part B called one sentence the whole of Exodus. At the foot of the plan Exodus gives it like this: “I will dwell in the midst of the children of Israel, and I will be their deity … who brought them out of the land of Egypt” (29:45–46). On the far side of chapter 19, Leviticus says it back: “I will be made holy in the midst of the children of Israel. I am YHWH who makes you holy, who brings you out of the land of Egypt to be your deity” (22:32–33).

In Exodus he dwells in their midst. In Leviticus he is made holy there.

Where the Pattern Is Thin

As before, here is where I would press.

The three rings are the plainest thing in this part, and one corner of them is soft. That the Levites take the tabernacle down and set it up is said in so many words, in Numbers. In Exodus it is made by “all the skilled” and set up by Moses, and the Levites’ service of it comes in only in the accounts (38:21). (The sons of Levi are in Exodus once more, with their swords, after the calf, 32:26–28. That is another matter.)

The matching of the columns starts from the two middles. It passes over chapters 13 to 15 and leaves the two ends of Leviticus uncovered. Each of those is a feature of Leviticus, found without Exodus in view. But everything in “Straight Down” depends on them, and a reader who doubts the layout of Leviticus has no reason to accept what was built on it.

The second column and the middle are the firm ones. Each has the column’s own subject under it, and a phrase that the two books use nowhere else. The last column is nearly as firm, in another way. It has no phrase of that kind, but each of its four units has something under it.

The first column is the weakest as a column. Its foot is carried out to the letter. For the rest, what Leviticus 8 to 12 share with it is that they are beginnings, and “beginnings” is a wider thing than what Part C said of the column. They are not about one man with the people somewhere else. The whole congregation is called to the door to watch (8:3–4).

The fourth column is fair. The road, with its bread and its quarrels, has nothing under it. And chapter 21 is about the priests’ own families.

Some things cross from one column to another. “Eye for eye” is in the cases, in the fourth column, and comes back in chapter 24, under the last. The oil for the lamp is commanded in the plan of the house, in the first column, and comes back in the same chapter.

The sentence about dwelling in their midst doesn’t keep to one column. In Exodus it is at the foot of the second. In Leviticus it comes under the last. And it comes once more out beyond the edge, in the closing chapters, with both its halves together: “I will set my dwelling in your midst … and I will be your deity, and you shall be my people” (26:11–12). The seventh year of the land is out there too (25:2–4), though Exodus has it in the last laws (23:10–11).

So the five groups we compared don’t hold everything that Leviticus takes up from Exodus. Its closing chapters gather some of it again.

And the turn from one to many would be there however the columns were matched. It is something the two books share. It does not show that the matching is right.

What Comes Next

Exodus ends with a house that has been built and filled with glory, and a people that has been gathered and has not yet moved. Moses has set out the bread, lit the lamps and offered on the altar. One order from the mountain is still waiting, the seven days of the priests, and with it everything the priests are to do from then on. Leviticus takes that up. The tabernacle that is finished in the last chapter of Exodus becomes, in Leviticus, the place a whole people’s life is arranged around, and in Numbers the centre of a camp on the march.

In Exodus he comes to dwell in their midst. What that asks of them is the next book’s business. The map of Exodus is the first part of something larger, and it was drawn to be gone on from.

From here the units are taken one at a time, beginning at the beginning, with a family that came down to Egypt and a king who did not know Joseph.
