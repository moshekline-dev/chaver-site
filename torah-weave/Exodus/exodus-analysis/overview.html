The map shows the nineteen literary units of Exodus, the passages whose beginning and end the text itself marks, as this introduction arranges them. Sixteen of the units fall into four groups of four, which we call quarters: Egypt; the road and the mountain; the plan of the tabernacle; and the building of the tabernacle. Three independent units stand between the quarters and belong to none of them: the sea (Unit 5), the covenant (Unit 10) and the tablets (Unit 15). Each unit on the map is linked to its own page, where its text is set out with its internal design. The four parts of this introduction show how the units are identified, how the map is built from them, and how it is read.

Introduction

Exodus reads like two books. For twenty-four chapters it tells a story, with a body of law set into its last chapters. A family that came down to Egypt grows into a people, is enslaved, and is brought out through the plagues and the sea to a mountain in the wilderness, where it receives laws and enters into a covenant. At chapter 25 the story stops. For the rest of the book the subject is a tabernacle, described first as it is shown to Moses on the mountain and then, after the episode of the golden calf, as the people make it. Much of the second description repeats the first. A reader may well ask why a story of deliverance and a set of building instructions belong to one book.

Exodus joins its two halves in a single sentence, which it gives twice. Before the first plague, YHWH tells Moses what to say to the people: “I will take you to me for a people, and I will be your deity, and you shall know that I am YHWH your deity, who brings you out from under the burdens of Egypt” (6:7). In the instructions for the tabernacle the sentence returns with something added: “I will dwell in the midst of the children of Israel, and I will be their deity. And they shall know that I am YHWH their deity, who brought them out of the land of Egypt to dwell in their midst” (29:45–46). The first time, the people are told that they will be brought out of Egypt. The second time, they are told what they were brought out for: so that YHWH might dwell in their midst.

The same events can therefore be described from two sides. For Israel, Exodus is a story of redemption: a people of slaves is brought out and made a nation. For YHWH, it is a story of revelation: again and again something is done so that Egypt, or Israel, shall “know that I am YHWH.” The story half of the book is chiefly about the people, and the tabernacle half is about a house for YHWH in their midst. Who acts runs the other way. In the half about the people, YHWH acts for them; in the half about YHWH, they act for him. He takes them out of a house, and they build him one. This introduction asks how Exodus holds the two together, and it finds the answer in the way the book is built.

This study shows that Exodus is made of nineteen literary units rather than forty chapters, and that the units are arranged in a definite plan. Sixteen of them form four quarters of four units each, and three independent units stand between the quarters. The plan has a feature that none of the other four books of the Torah shares: the four units of each quarter stand to one another as the four quarters of the book do, most closely in the first quarter and less exactly in the other three. The whole can be seen again, at a smaller size, in its parts.

Where Exodus Stands in the Torah

Each of the five books of the Torah is made of such units. The five books are in turn arranged in relation to one another, and the arrangement is not a simple row. Genesis, Leviticus and Deuteronomy set out their units in three rows, and the same plan can be followed from one of these books to the next. Together they form what we call the horizontal thread of the Torah Weave. Exodus and Numbers are built in a different way, but they share something with each other and with Leviticus. A story begins in Exodus, stops at Mount Sinai, and resumes in Numbers. A tabernacle is built at the end of Exodus, served throughout Leviticus, and prepared for travel at the beginning of Numbers. These three books form the vertical thread.

Leviticus belongs to both threads and stands where they meet. Exodus stands at the head of the vertical thread. All five books, with every one of their eighty-six units, are laid out on the map of the whole Torah at chaver.com.

A reader who comes to Exodus from Genesis should therefore not expect the three rows of Genesis. Exodus has a plan of its own, and finding that plan is the work of this introduction.

Methodology and Scope

This study employs close literary analysis of the Hebrew text in its received form. It attends to features that can be observed in the text itself: places where the book changes from one kind of writing to another, words that open a unit and return to close it, designs that run through a unit from one end to the other, repeated phrases, and the positions in which particular subjects recur. The approach is descriptive. It documents arrangements that exist in the text and asks what they bring into view. It does not reconstruct a history of how the text came to be.

The reading does not depend on any decision about who wrote the Torah, or when. Readers who approach Exodus as scripture and readers who approach it as an ancient book can follow the same words and test the same arrangements.

The scope is chiefly Exodus itself. Genesis is drawn on where Exodus continues what Genesis began. In Part D the plan of Exodus is followed out of the book, into Leviticus and Numbers. Verses are cited by the numbering of the Hebrew text; where English Bibles number a verse differently, the English number is given in brackets.

The Four Parts: Summary of the Argument

Part A: The Units of Exodus. Part A identifies the nineteen units. Exodus has no recurring formula of the kind that divides Genesis (“These are the generations of”), and its most frequent opening, “YHWH spoke to Moses, saying,” is used at divisions of every size. The identification therefore begins with the five places where the book stops one kind of writing and begins another: the opening of the judgments (21:1), the promise of a messenger at the end of the law (23:20), the plan of the tabernacle (25:1), the giving of the tablets (31:18), and the gathering of the people to build (35:1). Between these places each unit is found by its edges and by its internal design. Three passages that appear to be single wholes turn out to be two units each: the opening chapters, enclosed by two lists of the sons of Israel (1:1–5; 6:14–27); the ten plagues, whose account is closed after the ninth (11:10), with the tenth given an opening of its own (12:1); and the law, which divides where cases introduced by “when” and “if” give way to commands (22:17 [English 22:18]). When all the units have been found, they fall in the order four, one, four, one, four, one, four.

Part B: The Map of Exodus. Part B sets the nineteen units out as a map. The three independent units divide the book. The covenant (Unit 10) divides it into two halves, the story and the tabernacle, and the sea (Unit 5) and the tablets (Unit 15) each divide a half into two quarters. On either side of the sea and of the tablets stands something to be kept. Before the sea the Passover is to be kept “for your generations” (12:14), and after it a jar of manna is to be kept “for your generations” (16:32), so that on each side there is a bread to be remembered. Before the tablets and after them stands the Sabbath (31:16; 35:2). The covenant ceremony itself has the shape of the book: half of the blood is thrown on the altar and half on the people, and the book of the covenant is read between the two (24:6–8). The four quarters stand in a proportion: Egypt is to the road and the mountain as the plan is to the building, since in each half the whole people does what the first quarter gave to Moses alone. Egypt and the building complete each other, and the two quarters between them are the means by which the one becomes the other. Part B then reads the quarters one at a time and finds the same relations among the four units of each. Egypt repeats them most closely; the other three keep part of them, and in each the movement across a pair takes a form of its own. In every quarter the first pair of units concerns the people and the second what is YHWH’s. Across each pair, in Egypt and less exactly in the plan, a few become all the people; on the road and at the mountain the people go from having their needs met to being a holy nation; and in the building their separate gifts become one house that they share. In every quarter, too, the first unit and the last complete each other, with the two between as the means. At the four corners of the map stand four holy places, each with a line drawn around it: the ground at the bush (3:5), the mountain (19:12), the altar (29:37), and the tabernacle that Moses cannot enter (40:35).

Part C: Columns and Rows. Part C reads the same map along its long lines. Across the map, in five columns, the book moves from Moses alone to the whole people. In the units of the first column YHWH is with Moses and the people are elsewhere; the units of the last column are about the whole people. The four long columns stand to one another as the four quarters of the book do: the first and the last complete each other, and the two inner columns are the means by which the first becomes the last. The second column shows each of the people made YHWH’s, one at a time and in danger of death, from the firstborn on the night of the going out to the half shekel that each man pays as “a ransom for his life” (30:12). The fourth column shows them joined to one another, from the even share of manna to the half shekels cast into the sockets of the tabernacle (38:27). The middle column, which holds the sea, the covenant and the tablets, sets Moses and the whole people side by side, and at its centre the book of the covenant is read to the people and they take on YHWH’s law (24:7). That law is of two kinds: in the story, the law of the people among themselves; in the tabernacle, the ordered way of coming before YHWH. Down the map, in four rows, YHWH comes from the mountain into the midst of the people, and the wonders of the upper rows give way to holy days, holy things and a holy house in the lower ones.

Part D: Architecture and Meaning in Exodus. Part D follows the map out of the book. Both halves of Exodus lead beyond it: the story resumes in Numbers when the cloud lifts (Num 10:11–12), and the tabernacle is served in Leviticus. Taken together, Exodus, Leviticus and Numbers are arranged as the Israelite camp is arranged. The story, which concerns all Israel, is on the outside. Within it is the tabernacle as it is put up, taken down and carried: in Exodus the skilled make it and Moses sets it up, and in Numbers the Levites carry it, their work given “by the hand of Ithamar son of Aaron the priest” in the words of the accounts of Exodus (38:21; Num 4:28, 33). The service inside the tabernacle, which is the work of the priests, is at the centre. Part D then lays the map of Leviticus, as that book’s own commentary draws it, under the map of Exodus, middle to middle, and reads down the columns from one book into the next. The seven days of the priests’ consecration, commanded at the foot of the first column (29:35), are carried out in the units beneath it (Lev 8:33). The two inner columns of Exodus, the means, lie on the ring of Leviticus marked by the person and his relatives, and the first and the last on the ring marked by the seven days and the eighth. Beneath the middle column, where the people take on the law, lies the centre of Leviticus, chapter 19, addressed to “all the congregation of the children of Israel” (Lev 19:2). It holds both kinds of law, justice among the people and the ordered way of coming before YHWH, and there keeping the law makes the people holy, as YHWH is holy. Both books also turn at their middle from the single person to the community.

After the four parts, the units are taken one at a time, each with a commentary of its own.

Key Findings

Nineteen Units, Not Forty Chapters. Five changes in the kind of writing, together with the edges and internal designs of the units between them, divide Exodus into nineteen units. The units fall four, one, four, one, four, one, four.

Wholes That Are Pairs. The opening chapters, the ten plagues and the law each prove to be two units, set together as a pair.

Three Independent Units. The sea, the covenant and the tablets belong to none of the quarters. Each stands between two parts of the book. On either side of the tablets stands the Sabbath, and on either side of the sea a bread to be remembered for later generations; the covenant has the two halves of the book on its two sides.

The Covenant at the Centre. The unit at the middle of the book is the covenant between YHWH and the people. Its ceremony, with the blood divided between the altar and the people and the book read between them, has the shape of the book as a whole.

The Whole in Each Quarter. The four quarters of the book stand in a proportion: Egypt and the building complete each other, and the road and the mountain and the plan are the means between them. Each quarter repeats these relations among its own four units, Egypt most closely: a pair of units about the people followed by a pair about what is YHWH’s; a movement across each pair, which in Egypt is from a few to all the people and in the other quarters takes another form; and a first unit and a last that complete each other, with the two between as the means.

Four Holy Places at the Corners. The corners of the map hold the bush, the mountain, the altar and the tabernacle, each with a line drawn around it. At the mountain, whoever touches is put to death (19:12); at the altar, whoever touches becomes holy (29:37). The corners of the map of Genesis are likewise places of forbidden contact.

The Year at the End of Each Quarter. The last unit of every quarter speaks of the year (12:2; 23:10–17; 30:10; 40:17).

From Moses Alone to All Israel. Read across its columns, the map moves from YHWH present with Moses alone to YHWH present “in the sight of all the house of Israel” (40:38). Between the two, each of the people is made YHWH’s and they are joined to one another, and at the middle the people take on YHWH’s law, read to them from the book of the covenant.

From the Mountain into Their Midst. Read down its rows, the map moves from YHWH met at the mountain to YHWH dwelling in the midst of the people, and from wonders to holiness.

The Camp and the Next Book. Exodus, Leviticus and Numbers together are laid out as the camp is laid out, and the columns of Exodus continue into Leviticus, where the law taken on at the middle of Exodus is given at the middle of Leviticus.

Limitations and Open Questions

The arrangements described here are not all of equal strength, and Parts B, C and D each end by saying where their evidence is thin. The firmest findings are the plainest: the division of the book four, one, four, one, four, one, four; the two pairs in Egypt; the holy places at the corners; and the contrast between the first column and the last. Some pairs of units are held together more loosely than the pairs in Egypt, and the movement across each pair takes a somewhat different form in each quarter. The reading of the columns of Exodus into Leviticus depends on the plan of Leviticus set out in that book’s own commentary, and it is firmer in some columns than in others.

The study makes no claim about who composed the Torah or when. Its interpretations rest on the arrangements it describes, and a reader may accept the arrangements while disagreeing about what they mean.

Invitation to Engagement

The patterns documented here are observable, and any reader can test them against the Hebrew text. We invite readers to test the unit divisions against other possible divisions, to ask whether the relations found in each quarter are in fact the same relations, and to weigh whether the reading of the map follows from the evidence given for it. The map and the text of every unit are available at chaver.com for that purpose.

Part A: The Units of Exodus

A Stage-by-Stage Discovery

Introduction: Where Does the Book Divide?

Printed Bibles divide Exodus into forty chapters. The chapters are not part of the Torah. They were laid over the text many centuries after it was written, and they do not always fall where the text’s own divisions fall. Our first task is to find those divisions: the places where the Torah itself ends one literary unit and begins the next.

In Genesis this task was helped by a recurring formula. “These are the generations of” opens one section after another, and death notices close several of them. Exodus has no formula of that kind. Its most frequent opening, “YHWH spoke to Moses, saying,” does not mark divisions of any one size. In the plan of the tabernacle it opens a whole unit, then a section within the unit, and then a single instruction about a basin. It tells us that YHWH is about to speak to Moses, but not how much of the text his speech will cover.

Exodus does mark its divisions, but by other means. At some places the book stops writing one kind of material and begins another. At others, words that open a passage return to close it. Some passages have a design of their own that runs from one end to the other. And some units are known chiefly by their neighbours, because the unit before them has closed and the unit after them has begun. In two places a paragraph break in the Hebrew text confirms a boundary found by these other means. These are the same kinds of evidence that Genesis Part A called external markers, envelopes, internal structural perfection and adjacent boundaries.

One distinction has to be made before we begin. Passages may belong together without forming a single unit. A unit has two things at once: clear edges, and a single design that fills the space between them. We shall meet passages that appear to be a single whole and prove to be two units, because each half has a design and a close of its own. We shall also meet episodes that look separate and turn out to be one unit, because one design runs through all of them. At every boundary, therefore, we look at two things: what happens at the edge, and how the inside is built.

The full design of every unit is set out on its own page at chaver.com. In this part a unit’s design is given in outline wherever a boundary depends on it.

As in Genesis, we follow our progress on a grid. Each of the forty chapters has a square, and all of them begin empty. As the units are found, the chapters are marked. A chapter that lies wholly inside one unit receives an X. A chapter that two units share, because one unit ends partway through it and the next begins, receives a slash.

Verse numbers follow the Hebrew text as it is numbered in the Mechon Mamre edition. English Bibles number some verses differently (at the end of chapter 7 and in chapter 8, in chapter 20, and in chapters 21–22). Where the difference matters, the English number is given in brackets.

Stage One: Five Places Where the Writing Changes

Five times in the course of Exodus, the Torah stops one kind of writing and begins another.

The first is at the mountain. For twenty chapters the book has told a story. Then it says, “These are the judgments that you shall set before them” (21:1). The Torah no longer tells what happened. It prescribes what is to be done when a man strikes his neighbour, when an ox gores, or when someone leaves a pit uncovered.

The second comes two chapters later, where the law stops. Its last provision is the law against boiling a kid in its mother’s milk (23:19), a law that the Torah also uses to close the laws given with the second tablets (34:26) and the laws of food in Deuteronomy (Deut 14:21). The sentence that follows is not a law at all: “Behold, I send a messenger before you, to guard you on the way and to bring you to the place that I have prepared” (23:20). The same voice is speaking, but it now speaks of a road and a land, and it leads directly into the making of the covenant at the foot of the mountain.

The third comes when Moses enters the cloud on the mountain for forty days. The voice begins again, this time with a list of materials: gold, silver and bronze; blue, purple and scarlet yarn; linen, goats’ hair, skins and acacia wood; oil, spices and stones (25:1–7). Then comes the purpose of the list: “Let them make me a sanctuary, and I will dwell in their midst” (25:8). For nearly seven chapters after that, the tabernacle is the only subject.

The fourth comes where the description of the tabernacle ends: “And he gave to Moses, when he had finished speaking with him on Mount Sinai, the two tablets of the testimony” (31:18). The story resumes, and the story that resumes is the story of the golden calf.

The fifth comes after the tablets have been given a second time: “Moses assembled all the congregation of the children of Israel and said to them, These are the things that YHWH has commanded you to do” (35:1). From this point to the end of the book, everything that was commanded on the mountain is made.

These five places are our flags. It may be objected that the book changes its kind of writing in other places as well. The law of the Passover is given in Egypt, before the night on which the people leave, and the Ten Words are spoken at the mountain. The plan of the tabernacle, too, is spoken during Moses’ forty days on the mountain (24:18), so that it also lies within the story. The difference is one of scale and of form. The law of the Passover and the Ten Words are set within a single scene, and the scene goes on once they have been given: after the law of the Passover comes midnight, and the death of the firstborn of Egypt (12:29); after the Ten Words, the people stand at a distance in fear. At each of the five flags, by contrast, a new kind of writing begins with an opening of its own and runs on to the next flag, or to the end of the book.

Stage Two: The Story

We begin with the divisions that the story marks most plainly.

The book opens with a list of names: “These are the names of the sons of Israel who came to Egypt” (1:1), followed by Reuben, Simeon, Levi and the rest. Five chapters later, after Pharaoh has refused and the people’s labour has been made harder, the story stops for a second list: “These are the heads of their fathers’ houses” (6:14). Reuben comes first again, then Simeon, then Levi. At Levi the list slows down and traces the line from father to son until it reaches Aaron and Moses: “These are the Aaron and Moses to whom YHWH said, Bring the children of Israel out of the land of Egypt” (6:26).

A list of names stands at the beginning, and the same list is begun again in chapter 6. Whatever lies between the two lists belongs together.

The second division is plainer still. The ten plagues form a single series, from the river turned to blood to the death of the firstborn.

We therefore have two large pieces before any fine work has been done: from the first list of names to the second, and from the first plague to the last. Each of these pieces proves to be two units, and showing this requires a closer look.

The two lists of names

Between the two lists the story runs on for five chapters. A new king sets the people to hard labour and orders that their sons be thrown into the river. One of these sons is Moses. He is placed in the river in a basket and drawn out by Pharaoh’s daughter. He grows up, kills an Egyptian, and flees from Pharaoh to Midian (2:15). There he marries and keeps sheep, and one day he turns aside to look at a bush.

When the voice from the bush has finished speaking with him, Moses returns to his father-in-law and asks for leave to go back to his brothers in Egypt. Jethro answers, “Go in peace” (4:18).

The first unit ends here. Moses fled to Midian, and now Midian gives him leave to go.

The line could have been drawn elsewhere, for example where Moses flees or where he comes to the bush. It falls here because of the way the unit is built. In each of its three rows, the unit sets the people beside Moses, and both his flight and the scene at the bush fall within that design.

In the third row one verb, shama’, “hear,” stands on both sides. YHWH has heard the cry of the people (3:7), and Moses fears that the people will not hear his voice (4:1).

The next unit opens with a notice of place: “YHWH said to Moses in Midian, Go, return to Egypt” (4:19). Moses has been in Midian since he fled there (2:15), yet the Torah names the place all the same. A few verses later a second order is given, to match the first: “YHWH said to Aaron, Go to meet Moses in the wilderness” (4:27).

The brothers meet, gather the elders, and go in to Pharaoh. Pharaoh does not know YHWH and refuses to let the people go. He withholds the straw for the bricks, and the people, who believed Moses when he came to them (4:31), stop listening to him (6:9).

Then comes the second list of names, and after it a verse that again names a place: “And it was, on the day YHWH spoke to Moses in the land of Egypt” (6:28). Read straight on, this verse sounds like the beginning of the next sentence. The Hebrew text, however, sets a paragraph break after it, and what follows starts afresh, as if nothing had yet been said: “YHWH spoke to Moses, saying, I am YHWH; speak to Pharaoh king of Egypt all that I speak to you” (6:29).

The second unit began by naming a place, and at its far edge a place is named again. So the two lists of names enclose the first pair of units, and within the pair the line between the two units is drawn with the name of a place.¹

Nine plagues, and one

The ten plagues are not a single unit, because the text closes its account of them after the ninth.

The account begins with the fresh start of 6:29, and with Moses raising the same objection he raised before the list of names: “I am of uncircumcised lips; how will Pharaoh listen to me?” (6:30; compare 6:12). Then, before anything has happened, the text says: “Moses and Aaron did as YHWH commanded them; so they did” (7:6).

A staff becomes a serpent. The river turns to blood. Frogs, lice, swarms, the death of the cattle, boils, hail, locusts and darkness follow, nine plagues in all. A tenth is then announced: “One more plague I will bring on Pharaoh and on Egypt” (11:1). It does not come within this unit. What comes instead is a closing statement: “Moses and Aaron did all these wonders before Pharaoh” (11:10).

The account opened with “did as YHWH commanded” and closes with “did all these wonders.” It is closed with nine plagues done and the tenth still to come.

The tenth plague is given an opening of its own: “YHWH said to Moses and to Aaron in the land of Egypt” (12:1). This is the device we have already met. The second unit opened by naming the place where YHWH spoke, Midian, and this unit opens by naming the place where he spoke, the land of Egypt. In both cases a place is named at the line that divides a pair of units.

What follows is a single night, told in two ways at once: as it happened, and as it is to be kept in later generations. “I will pass through the land of Egypt on this night” (12:12). In the same speech: “This day shall be for you a remembrance, and you shall keep it as a feast to YHWH for your generations” (12:14). At midnight the firstborn of Egypt die, and the people are sent out in haste, with dough that has not had time to rise.

“It is a night of watching for YHWH, to bring them out of the land of Egypt; this night is YHWH’s, a watching for all the children of Israel for their generations” (12:42). The verse speaks in one breath of the night in Egypt and of the same night as it is to be kept in later generations.

The phrase “on this very day” (be-etsem ha-yom ha-zeh) occurs three times in Exodus, and all three are in this unit (12:17, 41, 51). The unit ends by looking far ahead, to a son who will one day ask what all this means (13:14), and to a sign to be worn on the hand (13:16).

Twice now, a passage that appears to be a single whole has proved to be two units: the chapters between the two lists of names, and the ten plagues. Two cases do not establish a rule. They do, however, raise the possibility that units in Exodus come in pairs, and Part B will take that possibility up.

The sea

When the night is over, the people are out of Egypt. The story then begins again, from Pharaoh’s side: “And it was, when Pharaoh sent the people out” (13:17).

The account of the crossing ends with Miriam and her drum, and the women dancing (15:21). The very next verse has the people leaving the place: “Moses made Israel journey from the Sea of Reeds” (15:22).

The sea therefore has an edge on each side of it. Before it is the night of the going out, which ended with the sign on the hand. After it is the road.

At the opening of the unit the concern is that the people will “see war” and return to Egypt (13:17). In the song at its close, YHWH is “a man of war” (15:3). The word “war” stands at both ends of the unit.

The road

On the road the narrative has a regular rhythm: the people journey, they arrive, and they complain.

They journey from the Sea of Reeds into the wilderness of Shur, and the water they find is bitter (15:22–23). They journey from Elim to the wilderness of Sin, and there is no bread (16:1). They journey on to Rephidim, and there is no water at all (17:1).

These might be three small units, one for each stop. But the three stops share one verb, nasah, “test.” At the first stop, YHWH shows Moses a piece of wood that makes the water sweet, and “there he set for them a statute and a judgment, and there he tested them” (15:25). At the second, YHWH sends bread from the sky “that I may test them, whether they will walk in my teaching or not” (16:4). At the third, Moses asks the people, “Why do you test YHWH?” (17:2), and the place is named Massah and Meribah, “because of the quarrelling of the children of Israel, and because they tested YHWH, saying, Is YHWH in our midst or not?” (17:7). Twice YHWH tests the people, and the third time the people test YHWH.

The verb shows that the three stops belong together. We have already seen, however, that belonging together is not enough to make one unit, since the ten plagues also belong together and form two units. What decides the matter is the design. The account of the plagues was closed after the ninth, and the tenth was given an opening of its own. On the road nothing is closed and nothing is opened. Each stop is told in the same two steps: first the complaint and its answer, and then what follows at that place. The three stops are set one under another, and that arrangement is the whole unit.

Jethro, and the mountain

The last unit of the story begins with an arrival: “Jethro, Moses’ father-in-law, came with his sons and his wife to Moses, into the wilderness where he was camped, at the mountain of the deity” (18:5).

A family visit and the thunder at Sinai seem at first to belong to different units. What joins them is what the visit leaves behind. Jethro finds Moses sitting from morning to evening, judging every dispute in Israel by himself, and he tells him that this cannot go on. Lesser matters are to go to judges. Moses is to keep one task for himself: “You be for the people before the deity, and bring the matters to the deity. And you shall warn them of the statutes and the teachings” (18:19–20). Moses takes the advice, and judges are appointed.

Then the people come to the mountain, and the text marks the arrival with a date and a journey: “In the third month after the children of Israel had gone out of the land of Egypt, on this day they came to the wilderness of Sinai” (19:1); “They journeyed from Rephidim … and Israel camped there, facing the mountain” (19:2). A date and a journey are the kinds of marker we have been watching for, and a new scene does begin here. The question is why it does not begin a new unit.

The answer is that the new scene falls within an account that Jethro’s visit has already begun, and the thread of that account is Moses’ position. At the mountain YHWH gives Moses words for the people. Moses carries them down, and then “Moses brought the words of the people back to YHWH” (19:8). YHWH tells him why it is done this way: “so that the people may hear when I speak with you, and believe in you for ever” (19:9). On the third day the mountain shakes, and “Moses spoke, and the deity answered him with a voice” (19:19). Then the Ten Words are spoken to the whole people, and it is more than they can bear. They say to Moses, “You speak with us, and we will hear; but let not Elohim speak with us, lest we die” (20:15 [English 20:19]). And so it is done: “The people stood far off, and Moses drew near to the thick darkness where the deity was” (20:17 [English 20:21]).

This is the position that Jethro gave him. From the visit to the end of the unit, Moses stands between the people and the deity, and each scene places him there again.

Stage Three: The Law and the Covenant

The law appears to be a single whole, as plainly as the ten plagues did.

It lies between two flags. It has one heading, “These are the judgments,” and no second heading anywhere within it. It also begins and ends with the same count. Its first law concerns a slave: “six years he shall serve, and in the seventh he shall go out free” (21:2). Among its last laws is one concerning the land: “six years you shall sow your land … and in the seventh you shall release it” (23:10–11).

Yet the law, too, proves to be two units, divided at the point where the form of the laws changes.

The first part consists almost entirely of damages and cases: “When a man …”; “If …”. A slave is due his freedom. One man kills another, or two men quarrel. An ox gores, a pit is left open, a fire spreads into a neighbour’s field. Something left in a neighbour’s keeping disappears. These are the laws of people who live close together and come into conflict.

This first part has a design of its own. Its cases come in pairs, two cases that answer each other set side by side, and two pairs make a row, five rows in all.

It also makes four units since the sea, two of story and two of law, and the four share one subject. At the first stop on the road, YHWH “set for them a statute and a judgment” (15:25). At the mountain Jethro found Moses making known “the statutes of the deity and his teachings” (18:16). In the law we now have the judgments themselves. What was set at the bitter water has been given in full.

The covenant

The law ends with the kid in its mother’s milk. What follows is the promise of a messenger, and at first it looks like an appendix to the law, a few words of encouragement before the next subject. It is in fact the first of three parts of a single unit.

The unit has three parts, and each of the three has three sections.

In the first part YHWH speaks to the whole people, and each of its three sections opens with something sent ahead of them: “I send a messenger before you” (23:20); “my messenger will go before you” (23:23); “I will send the hornet before you” (23:28). The middle section also sends one thing more before it closes: “my terror” (23:27).

In the second part, Moses is told to come up with Aaron, Nadab, Abihu and seventy of the elders, and to bow down at a distance; Moses alone is to come near, and the people are not to come up at all (24:1–2). Moses then tells the people all the words of YHWH and all the judgments, and the people answer with one voice, “All the words that YHWH has spoken we will do” (24:3). The covenant is made with an altar, a book and basins of blood (24:4–8). Then the elders go up, “and they saw the deity of Israel,” and they ate and drank (24:9–11).

In the third part Moses goes on alone. He is called up to receive the tablets (24:12–13). The elders are told to wait, and the cloud covers the mountain (24:14–15). The glory of YHWH rests on Mount Sinai, and Moses enters the cloud (24:16–18).

The whole people is at the foot of the mountain, a few go partway up, and Moses alone reaches the top.

Stage Four: The Plan and the Tablets

Moses is in the cloud, and for seven chapters one voice speaks without any answer. It describes a tabernacle, piece by piece. We call this section the plan.

Each of the first two units of the plan is enclosed by its own words: what the unit says at its beginning, it says again at its end, and the repetition marks where the unit stops.

The first begins with an order for Moses to pass on to the people: “Speak to the children of Israel, that they take me an offering” (25:2). Then come the ark, the table and the lampstand; the curtains, boards and bars; the veil and the screen; the altar and the court. Three times Moses is reminded that he is to make all of this as it was shown to him on the mountain (25:40; 26:30; 27:8). The unit ends with a second order to pass on: “You shall command the children of Israel, that they take to you pure olive oil” (27:20). The weekly reading in the synagogue begins a new portion at this verse, and the next unit opens in a similar way, “And you, bring near to you Aaron your brother” (28:1). The order about the oil belongs with the house, however, because it repeats the opening of the unit: in both verses Moses is to tell the children of Israel “that they take.” Its last verse gives the place the name that it will bear for the rest of the book: the tent of meeting (27:21).

The second unit turns from the house to the men who will serve in it, and specifically to what they will wear. There are holy garments for Aaron, “for glory and for beauty” (28:2): an ephod, a breastpiece, a robe hung with bells, and a gold plate for his forehead. And there are garments for his sons, “for glory and for beauty” (28:40). In this case a unit and a chapter coincide.

Stage Five: The Building

Moses comes down, gathers the whole people, and speaks first of the Sabbath: “Six days work shall be done, but the seventh day shall be holy to you” (35:2). The Sabbath was the last subject spoken of on the mountain before the tablets were given. It is now the first subject spoken of after them.

Then Moses asks for the materials. He repeats the list that opened the plan, and the people go away and return carrying what they have. The women spin, the leaders bring the stones, and Bezalel and Oholiab are put in charge of the work. The unit ends with a problem that builders seldom face. The craftsmen come to Moses and ask him to stop the giving, for “the material was enough for all the work, and more than enough” (36:7).

Then the work begins: “All the skilled among those doing the work made the tabernacle” (36:8). The unit describes the making of the curtains, the boards and the veil; the ark, the table and the lampstand; the two altars, the basin and the court; down to the tent pegs (38:20). The makers do not follow the order of the plan. On the mountain the ark came first and the tent afterwards, while the makers begin with the tent.

This is the only unit in Exodus in which neither YHWH nor Elohim is named, from its first verse to its last. The name is heard just before the unit begins, when the craftsmen speak of “the work that YHWH commanded” (36:5), and again just after it ends, when we are told that Bezalel “made all that YHWH commanded Moses” (38:22). Within the unit there is only “he made,” again and again. The absence of the divine name begins where the unit begins and ends where it ends.

The next unit announces itself with a heading: “These are the accounts of the tabernacle” (38:21).

First the metals are counted, and the sum can be checked. Each man who was counted paid half a shekel, and 603,550 men were counted (38:26). That comes to 301,775 shekels. The text gives the silver as a hundred talents and 1,775 shekels (38:25), and at three thousand shekels to the talent this is the same figure. The hundred talents went into a hundred sockets, “a talent to a socket,” and the remaining 1,775 shekels into hooks and bands (38:27–28). The account balances to the shekel.

The account then moves from the metals to the yarn: “And from the blue and the purple and the scarlet they made garments of service … and they made the holy garments for Aaron, as YHWH commanded Moses” (39:1). The yarn becomes the clothes, so the reckoning continues directly into the making of them: the ephod, the breastpiece, the robe and the gold plate. Each garment is followed by the same words, “as YHWH commanded Moses.”

The giving speaks several times of what “YHWH commanded” (35:4, 10, 29; 36:1), and the making of the house does not speak of it at all. What begins with the accounts, in the verse about Bezalel (38:22), is the report that a thing was done “as YHWH commanded Moses,” and that report follows each finished garment. That is what makes the accounts and the clothes a single unit: one reckoning of what was used, from the gold down to the yarn, and one refrain throughout.

The refrain is not what ends the unit, however, since it continues beyond it. The unit is ended by the next sentence: “All the work of the tabernacle of the tent of meeting was finished” (39:32). The making is over. From this point nothing more is made. The finished things are brought to Moses, he is told where each one belongs, and he puts it in its place.

This last unit is framed by the words with which Genesis closes the account of creation. At the end of the sixth day, “Elohim saw all that he had made, and behold, it was very good” (Gen 1:31). Then “the heavens and the earth were finished (va-yekhullu),” and “Elohim finished (va-yekhal) on the seventh day his work (melakhto),” and “Elohim blessed the seventh day” (Gen 2:1–3). The same verbs, in the same roles, close the making of the tabernacle.

The question is whether Exodus is nineteen units laid end to end, or a structure that has to be seen as a whole. Part B begins with that question.

Part B: The Map of Exodus

From Units to Architecture

Introduction: Why One Book?

The overview asked why a story of deliverance and the instructions for a tabernacle should make one book. Part A gave the question a sharper form. It found nineteen units in the order four, one, four, one, four, one, four, and it ended by asking whether they lie end to end or form a structure that has to be seen as a whole.

The single units in that order are the independent units, the sea (Unit 5), the covenant (Unit 10) and the tablets (Unit 15). The covenant stands where the story ends and the tabernacle begins, the sea in the middle of the story and the tablets in the middle of the tabernacle half. However unlike the two halves of Exodus are, they are built alike.

Stage One: Drawing the Map

The book opens with a list: “And these are the names of the sons of Israel who came to Egypt” (1:1). At the end of the second unit the list comes back, now as “the heads of their fathers’ houses” (6:14). This time it does not run through all the sons. It stops at the third, Levi, and follows his line down until it reaches Aaron and Moses (6:16–26). Part A found that these two lists close the first two units in like a pair of brackets, and that the next two units share the ten plagues between them, nine in the third and the tenth in the fourth, on the night of the going out. The four units in Egypt come in two pairs.

On the map of Genesis, units that come in pairs are set side by side, and the next pair beneath them, as lines run down a page. We draw Exodus in the same way, beginning with its largest parts, the four groups of four units, which we call quarters. The two quarters of the story stand side by side, Egypt and then the road and the mountain. The two quarters of the tabernacle stand beneath them, the plan and then the building. A covenant has two parties and something that binds them. In Exodus the parties are the subjects of its two halves, the people and YHWH, and this unit stands between the halves as the reading of the book stood between the altar and the people. The ceremony takes the two parties in the other order, the altar first and the people last; in both, the two are bound across a middle. Here the question with which we began finds its answer. A story of a people and a house for YHWH are one book because they are the two sides of one covenant.

Stage Three: One Sentence, Four Quarters

In Egypt, before the first plague, YHWH gives Moses a sentence to carry to the people: “I will take you to me for a people, and I will be your deity” (6:7). The story makes the first clause come true, and when the people reach the mountain YHWH can say that it has: “I carried you on eagles’ wings and brought you to me” (19:4). The plan of the tabernacle takes up the second clause almost word for word: “I will dwell in the midst of the children of Israel, and I will be their deity” (29:45). Each of the two parties to the covenant is given a half of the book, and this one sentence holds the whole book.

On the mountain Moses alone is shown a pattern: “According to all that I show you, the pattern of the tabernacle and the pattern of all its vessels, so you shall make it” (25:9). When the building begins he gathers “all the congregation of the children of Israel” (35:1), and at the end the finished work is brought to him, done “according to all that YHWH commanded Moses” (39:42). What Moses saw, all the people make.

The story moves in the same way, and it says so in advance. At the bush Moses is given a sign: “When you bring the people out of Egypt, you shall serve the deity on this mountain” (3:12). The sign names both parts of the story, the going out and the coming to this mountain, and it is given to Moses alone. When they arrive, “all the people answered together and said, All that YHWH has spoken we will do” (19:8). In each half of the book, the second quarter shows the whole people doing what the first quarter told to Moses alone.

Egypt is a house as well. The people are brought out of “the house of slaves” (13:3), and at the mountain YHWH names himself by that deliverance: “I am YHWH your deity, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slaves” (20:2). Between the two, the last verse of the last laws already speaks of a house: “The first of the first fruits of your land you shall bring to the house of YHWH your deity” (23:19). YHWH takes the people out of one house in the first half of the book, and in the second they build him another to dwell in.

The four quarters therefore stand in a proportion: Egypt is to the road and the mountain as the plan is to the building, since in each half the whole people does what the first quarter gave to Moses alone. In such a proportion the first and the last terms are the extremes, and the two between them are the means. Egypt and the building are the extremes, and they complete each other, as the work and its finishing have shown. The road and the mountain and the plan are the means by which the one becomes the other. Before the people can build a house for YHWH, they must take on his word, and Moses must be shown what is to be made. At the mountain the people say, “All that YHWH has spoken we will do (na’aseh)” (19:8). On the mountain Moses is told, “so you shall make it (ta’asu)” (25:9). When the building begins, Moses gathers the people with both: “These are the things that YHWH has commanded you to do (la’asot)” (35:1). At the end both are fulfilled in one verse: “According to all that YHWH commanded Moses, so the children of Israel did (asu) all the work” (39:42). In Hebrew the doing and the making are one verb.

So the four quarters are bound together in three ways. The first two concern the people and the last two YHWH’s house; in each half, what was told to Moses alone is taken up by the whole people; and the first and the last complete each other, with the two between as the means. If the whole book is reflected in its parts, the four units of each quarter should be bound together in the same three ways. Egypt, where the four units are already known to come in two pairs, is the place to begin.

Stage Five: The Other Three Quarters

After Egypt comes the sea, and after the sea the same order begins again. Each of the three later quarters can be held up against Egypt, and two of the relationships found there recur: in every quarter the first two units belong to the people and the last two to YHWH, and in every quarter the first unit and the last complete each other, with the two between as the means.

The movement within each pair takes two forms. In Egypt the people had to be gathered. Moses was alone at the bush, the people did nothing through nine plagues, and only on the last night did every household act. The plan widens in the same way, from a few to all the people, and on the map it stands beneath Egypt, on the left. In the two quarters on the right, the road and the mountain and the building, the people are already gathered. On the road and at the mountain the movement is from meeting their needs to becoming a holy nation; in the building, from their separate gifts to one house that they share.

The road and the mountain (Units 6–9). Jethro arrives at the mountain and watches Moses sit from morning to evening while the people stand around him (18:13). Moses explains: “When they have a matter, it comes to me, and I judge between a man and his neighbour (bein ish u-vein re’ehu)” (18:16). Jethro tells him to appoint judges for the small matters, to hear the great ones himself, and to “bring the matters to the deity (el ha-elohim)” (18:19, 22). In the unit that follows, the law begins: “These are the judgments that you shall set before them” (21:1). Case after case is between a man and his neighbour, from men who quarrel and come to blows (21:18) to silver left in a neighbour’s keeping (22:6 [English 22:7]), and when the facts cannot be known, “the matter of both shall come to the judges” (ad ha-elohim, 22:8 [English 22:9]); the Hebrew is the same word in both places. The story sets up the courts, and the law tells them what to judge.

That is also where the quarter divides. The story is full of people, and the two units of law name none of them, not Moses and not even Israel. Only the voice that began at the mountain is heard, and in the last laws it speaks in the first person: “If he cries out to me, I will surely hear his cry” (22:22 [English 22:23]). The plan’s first pair, the house and the garments, is what the people are to make, and it opens by asking for gifts “from every man whose heart moves him” (25:2). Its second pair is what is to be done before YHWH.

Here the widening is Egypt’s, from a few to all the people, though less exactly. The house and the garments are both shown to Moses alone, and between them the only widening is in the names: no tribe is named in the house, while the garments carry all twelve, cut in the stones that Aaron bears over his heart (28:21, 29). In the second pair it is plain. The seven days are for Aaron and his sons. The service that follows is “for your generations” (29:42), and its silver comes from every man who is counted: “the rich shall not give more, and the poor shall not give less, than half a shekel” (30:15).

At the beginning of the quarter the people are to act: “Let them make me a sanctuary, and I will dwell in their midst” (25:8). In its last unit YHWH acts: “I will make holy the tent of meeting and the altar, and Aaron and his sons I will make holy, to be priests to me. And I will dwell in the midst of the children of Israel, and I will be their deity” (29:44–45). The people make the house, and YHWH makes it holy. The promise that opened the plan, “I will dwell (ve-shakhanti) in their midst,” returns in its last unit. The verse in which he makes them holy also names the means: Aaron and his sons, whose garments are the second unit of the quarter and whose seven days are the third.

The building (Units 16–19). Two refrains divide the quarter’s pairs. In the giving the heart is named thirteen times, and in all the making of the house YHWH is not named once. The first pair is what the people do with their hearts and their hands, the second a command carried out.

In the next unit the yarn has become curtains, joined with fifty clasps, “and the tabernacle was one” (36:13). That is the movement in this quarter, from what belonged to each person to one thing for all. The second pair moves the same way: the garments are made one by one for Aaron and his sons, and at the end the tabernacle stands whole “in the sight of all the house of Israel” (40:38).

That each corner unit holds, within its own quarter, the place its quarter holds in the book follows from the way the map is drawn. What stands in those four places does not.

At the mountain, “whoever touches” (kol ha-noge’a) dies. At the altar, “whoever touches” becomes holy. Exodus uses the phrase of the mountain, the altar and the anointed vessels (30:29), and of nothing else. Moses stands at both of the other two corners. At the bush Moses is told not to come near, and at the tabernacle he cannot come in. The book opens and closes with Moses stopped at a line.

The corners of the map of Genesis hold scenes of one kind as well. In the stories of the fathers, each of the four corners holds a woman of the family exposed to a foreign ruler or his son, and a contact that is forbidden. Sarah is taken into Pharaoh’s house, and YHWH strikes Pharaoh for it; the Hebrew says that he “touched” him with great “touches” (va-yenagga’ … nega’im, Gen 12:17). Sarah is taken again, by Abimelech, who “had not come near her” (Gen 20:4). Rebekah is passed off as Isaac’s sister, and when Abimelech learns the truth he commands all his people: “Whoever touches this man or his wife shall surely be put to death” (Gen 26:11). At the fourth corner no one prevents it. Shechem, the son of the prince of the land, takes Dinah and lies with her (Gen 34:2), and her brothers Simeon and Levi “came upon the city … and killed every male” (Gen 34:25).

The two top corners of Exodus take up the same words. “Do not come near” (3:5) is the verb of Abimelech, who “had not come near” Sarah. “Whoever touches the mountain shall surely be put to death” (19:12) is Abimelech’s command about Rebekah and Isaac, nearly word for word, with the mountain in place of the man and his wife. In Genesis the corners are places where a person must not be touched, and the one time the contact takes place, a city dies. In Exodus it is a holy place that draws the line.

Where the Pattern Is Thin

The observations on which the map rests are not all equally strong. The firmest are the plainest: the book divides four, one, four on each side of the covenant; Egypt falls into two pairs by its two lists of names and by its nine plagues and one; and at each corner of the map there is a holy place with a line drawn around it. Egypt is the quarter in which the whole book is seen most clearly, and the other three repeat it less exactly. That the two in the middle are the means is said in advance only in Egypt, at the bush (3:19–20); elsewhere, in the book as in its other quarters, it is our reading of what they do. On the road and at the mountain YHWH comes down and speaks in the first pair, and the second pair is his because the law is his. In the plan the first pair is the house in which YHWH is to dwell, and it is the people’s because they are to make it. The clothes as what is seen of the priests is a reading; they are also “holy garments” (28:2), and Aaron wears them “when he goes into the holy place … before YHWH” (28:29). In the square of the four quarters the top row rests on less than the bottom: that Egypt is told to Moses alone rests on the sign of 3:12, in a quarter full of people.

Of the four corners, the altar stands out least within its own quarter, because the whole plan is holy. The mountain is also fenced outside its quarter, at the covenant (24:1–2) and at the tablets (34:3), but neither of those units uses the word “holy,” and each of the four corners does. “Touch” and “come near” are ordinary words; what carries the comparison with Genesis is a whole sentence, “whoever touches … shall surely be put to death,” said by a king of Gerar about a man and his wife and by YHWH about a mountain. The year does not stand at the end of every quarter with equal weight, since the plan has only “once in the year” (30:10), and the feasts are listed once more in the unit of the tablets (34:18–24), which ends no quarter.

What Comes Next

Part A asked whether the nineteen units are laid end to end or form a whole. Drawn as a map, they hold together. The covenant divides the book into two halves and has the shape of the whole, and the sea and the tablets divide the halves into quarters. Egypt repeats the bonds among the four quarters closely; the other three repeat them less exactly.

We have read the map one quarter at a time. Once the quarters are set in a square, however, their units also run in long lines across the whole map. The sea, the covenant and the tablets make a column of their own down the middle, with two columns of units on either side. At the head of the first column Moses stands alone before a bush that burns with fire. At the foot of the last, the cloud is on the tabernacle by day and fire is in it by night, “in the sight of all the house of Israel” (40:38).

Part C reads the map by its columns and its rows, and asks what the units in each line have in common.

What was promised to Moses alone in Unit 1 has happened to everyone in Unit 7.

The next pair seems to have nothing in common: Unit 3, the nine plagues, and Unit 9, the last laws. Unit 3, though, takes a kingdom apart a step at a time. It begins in Pharaoh’s court with a staff that becomes a serpent and harms no one (7:10). When the locusts are announced, his own servants ask him, “Do you not yet know that Egypt is lost?” (10:7). YHWH tells Pharaoh to his face what it is all for: “so that you may know that I am YHWH in the midst of the land” (8:18 [English 8:22]). In Unit 2 Pharaoh had said, “I do not know YHWH” (5:2). Egypt is the country where YHWH is not known, and it comes apart.

The laws of Unit 9 go the other way. Most of them are spoken to “you,” a whole people. They begin with those who are to have no place in the nation at all: “You shall not let a sorceress live” (22:17 [English 22:18]). They end with a whole nation keeping one calendar: “Three times in the year you shall keep a feast to me” (23:14). Between the two the weak are given a place, judges and a court are set over the people, and the nation is built up a step at a time. Writing on this unit with Paul Hocking, we called what it portrays “a process of ‘socialization,’ or ‘nation-building.’”¹ Inside it YHWH speaks in the first person. Of the poor man’s cloak he says, “When he cries out to me, I will hear, for I am gracious” (22:26 [English 22:27]). In the first column a state where YHWH is not known is taken apart; in the last a nation is put together with YHWH speaking in it.

The clause is the same, first as a command and then as a fact. The first column has the pieces, and the last has the whole.

The cases of Unit 8 are about what happens when sharing is not easy. They concern “a man” and “his neighbour” (re’ehu), as when one man’s ox gores his neighbour’s ox (21:35). From the case of the open pit on (21:34), case after case is settled by one verb, “he shall pay” (yeshallem), from the root that means “whole.” What one man has broken, he makes whole for the other.

In Unit 5 it is YHWH himself, “YHWH went before them by day in a pillar of cloud” (13:21). In Unit 10 it is his messenger, “my messenger shall go before you” (23:23). In Unit 15 it is what the people ask Aaron to make when Moses is late in coming down: “Make us a deity who will go before us” (32:1). Where the two sides of the map meet, someone leads the whole people on its way. At the middle of the middle column stands Unit 10, the covenant between YHWH and the people.

At the centre of that unit, in the middle cell of its three rows of three, a book is read. Moses has told the people “all the words of YHWH and all the judgments” (24:3), the word with which the cases of Unit 8 began (21:1), and he has written them down (24:4). Then he reads the book of the covenant in the hearing of the people (24:7), the scene that Part B set between the altar and the people. Before it, at the sea, the people “feared YHWH” (14:31), and no law is given. After it, in Unit 15, the words of the covenant are written again, “Write for yourself these words” (34:27). Among them is the close of the last laws, the feasts and the first fruits, nearly word for word (34:18–26; 23:14–19). Beside it are laws that Unit 4 gave to be told: the unleavened bread, and the firstborn of a donkey redeemed with a lamb (34:18–20; 13:12–13). Down the middle column a people that fears YHWH comes to hold his law in writing.

Across the Map

The four long columns are related as the four units of a quarter are, and as the four quarters of the book are. The first and the last complete each other. Moses was shown the pattern on the mountain (25:9), and at the end he “saw all the work, and behold, they had done it as YHWH commanded” (39:43). The two inner columns, one on each side of the middle, are the means by which the first becomes the last.

In the inner column on the left each of the people becomes YHWH’s, one at a time and in danger of death. Its law is told, from a father to his son, and kept “in your mouth” (13:9). It also names what the making is for: the clothes are to be “for glory and for beauty” (28:2).

Between the two inner columns, at the middle, the book of the covenant is read, and the people who feared YHWH at the sea take on his law. What was “in your mouth” is now “the teaching and the commandment which I have written” (24:12). No unit to the left of the middle has the verb “write.” To the right, the gold plate for Aaron’s forehead, “engraved” in the plan (28:36), is made when “they wrote on it a writing” (39:30). What was spoken is realized when it is written, as what was shown to Moses on the mountain is realized when it is built.

On the left, the danger of death to the one who comes before YHWH is warded off: “there shall be no plague among you” (12:13), “that soul shall be cut off” (12:15), “so that he does not die” (28:35). The first two are found in no other column. The Hebrew of the third, ve-lo yamut, comes also at 9:4, where nothing of Israel’s shall die in the plague, and at 21:18, where a man struck in a quarrel does not die; only here is it the reason for what is done. On the right, the law the people have taken on protects them from one another. A life taken is answered “life for life” (21:23); what one man breaks he makes whole for the other; and the stranger is not to be wronged, “for you were strangers in the land of Egypt” (22:20 [English 22:21]).

In the inner column on the right the people come to belong to one another. The ransom given to YHWH on the left (30:12) is given here because of a neighbour (21:30). What the plan ordered “for glory and for beauty,” the people’s gifts and work bring into being: “they made … the holy garments for Aaron, as YHWH commanded Moses” (39:1).

The law taken on at the middle is of two kinds. In the story it is the law of the people among themselves. In the tabernacle it is the ordered way of coming before YHWH, which the people bring into being by building. The word that opened the cases, “these are the judgments” (21:1), is heard there too. Aaron wears “the breastpiece of judgment” (28:15), and in it he is to carry “the judgment (mishpat) of the children of Israel on his heart before YHWH continually” (28:30). Even the tabernacle is to be set up “according to its judgment (ke-mishpato)” (26:30). The bells hung “so that he does not die” (28:35) are made on the right “as YHWH commanded Moses” (39:26), and there death is not named.

The middle column makes a covenant over each. Exodus says that a covenant is cut only in Units 10 and 15. At the covenant YHWH cuts it “over all these words” (24:8), the judgments that Moses has just told the people (24:3); they end with the feasts, “three times in the year all your males shall appear before the Lord YHWH” (23:17). In the tablets he cuts it again, “according to these words” (34:27), and now all the words are of worship: the feasts, the firstborn and the first fruits, with the same three appearances in the year (34:23). The first covenant stands at the end of the story, over the law of the people among themselves, with worship at its close; the second stands in the middle of the tabernacle half, over the way of coming before YHWH.

The Map Read Both Ways

Across the map what was given to Moses alone becomes the whole people’s, and down it YHWH comes to them.

Part B found that one sentence holds the whole book. Its first clause, “I will take you to me for a people” (6:7), comes true in the story. Its second, “and I will be your deity,” is taken up in the plan: “I will dwell in the midst of the children of Israel, and I will be their deity” (29:45). On the map each clause also runs through the whole. Taking a people is what the columns do, from Moses alone, by way of the law read at the middle, to all the house of Israel. And what was shown to Moses on the mountain is, at the end, the house the people built. Dwelling in their midst is what the rows do, from the mountain to the tent in the camp. Read both ways, the map holds a people and a dwelling in the midst of it. YHWH and the people of Israel are joined, by a covenant, and by a house in the midst of the camp.

Where the Pattern Is Thin

The observations of this part are not all equally strong. The firmest is the first column against the last. Every unit of the one has Moses with YHWH and the people elsewhere, and every unit of the other is about the people as a whole. Unit 1, though, begins with the whole people before it narrows to Moses. Unit 17, in which the house is made, is the one unit of Exodus that names neither YHWH nor Elohim; it stands in the last column because of who does the work.

Units 3 and 9, the plagues and the last laws, share almost no words. What they share is a movement in opposite directions, the one taking a country apart and the other putting one together. Without the movement there is no pair.

In the tabernacle half the columns mostly answer each other as plan and carrying out, and that would be so however the map were drawn. What the map adds is which unit carries out which, and two things do not fall where they should. The request for gifts that opens the house (25:1–9) is carried out in Unit 16, in the fourth column. The incense altar and the basin of the daily service (30:1–10, 17–21) are made in Unit 17, in the last (37:25–29; 38:8).

The second column rests a good deal on one reading, that the lodging place and the night of the going out are two forms of one scene; the text does not connect them in so many words. That the people become YHWH’s in the second column and are joined to one another in the fourth is our way of saying what each column holds. For the second column the wording rests on the text’s own “to me” and “mine” (13:2; 28:1); for the fourth the text has no word of its own. Some things also fall on the wrong side. The line between Israel and Egypt is drawn most plainly in the plagues, in the first column (8:18 [English 8:22]; 9:4; 11:7), and “I will dwell in the midst of the children of Israel” (29:45) stands in the second. Death, too, is not the second column’s alone. At the mountain the whole people is in danger of coming too near, “lest we die” (20:15 [English 20:19]), and the sentence “he shall be put to death” (yumat) is found on both sides of the middle (21:12; 31:14–15).

Moses and the people side by side are the subject of the middle column but not a mark of its own, for Unit 7 also has them so: “the people stood far off, and Moses drew near to the thick darkness” (20:17 [English 20:21]). What only the middle units have is someone walking before the people. Exodus does not say what the book of the covenant holds; that it holds the cases and the last laws rests on “the judgments” (24:3; 21:1). The judgment that Aaron carries on his heart (28:30) is a decision sought by the Urim and Thummim, not a body of law. The second covenant comes after the first has been broken at the calf, and its tablets hold “the words that were on the first tablets” (34:1); that the two covenants are over two kinds of law rests on the words each is cut over. Nor is the writing to the right of the middle law: the memorial about Amalek (17:14) and the words on the gold plate. The two inner columns are means as columns, not unit by unit along a row; the priests’ clothes in Unit 12 do not lead to the house being made in Unit 17.

The rows are a matter of weight, not a clean cut. There is a good deal that is holy in the top row: the ground at the bush, the Sabbath of the manna (16:23), the people made ready at the mountain (19:10). The cloud in Unit 19 is a wonder if anything is. Unit 8 says very little of YHWH (21:13–14; 22:10 [English 22:11]). Unit 11, the plan of the house, in the third row, is YHWH’s own speech from the mountain, and it belongs to that row by what it is about, a place for the people to make. And the word of the question at Rephidim comes back outside the rows, in the tablets: “I will not go up in your midst” (33:3, 5), and Moses’ plea, “let the Lord go in our midst” (34:9).

What Comes Next

We have now read the map three ways: a quarter at a time, by its columns, and by its rows. The same nineteen units were in front of us each time, and nothing was moved.

One thing is left before the units are taken one at a time. Exodus stops, but it does not come to an end. In its last unit the people are still camped at the mountain, and the tabernacle has been set up, but the making of its priests has been commanded and not yet told. Part D follows the map out of the book, to see where its two halves go, and what lies beneath its columns in Leviticus.

Part D: Architecture and Meaning in Exodus

Where the Two Halves Go

Part B asked why a story and a building should make one book, and found the answer in the covenant: a people’s story on one side, a house for YHWH on the other, and the covenant between the two parties. Part C ended on something left undone. When Exodus stops, the tabernacle stands, but the making of its priests has been commanded and not yet told. The book stops, but it does not come to an end, and both of its halves lead out of it.

The story stops first. “In the third month after the children of Israel went out of the land of Egypt … Israel camped there, facing the mountain” (19:1–2). From that verse in Unit 7 to the last verse of the book, the people do not move. The last verse speaks of “all their journeys” (40:38), but as what will be, not what has been. The journey begins again two books later, in Numbers Unit 3: “In the second year, in the second month, on the twentieth of the month, the cloud lifted from over the tabernacle of the testimony, and the children of Israel set out on their journeys from the wilderness of Sinai” (Num 10:11–12).

The building ends with Moses outside it. The glory fills the tabernacle, and “Moses could not come into the tent of meeting” (40:35). The next book opens: “He called to Moses, and YHWH spoke to him from the tent of meeting” (Lev 1:1). The cloud that kept Moses out has become a voice calling him from within.

Leviticus stays inside, as far in as the place within the veil, where Aaron may come only once a year (Lev 16:2, 34), and from there it turns outward again, to the people who live with that tent in their midst. At the beginning of Numbers the tabernacle has become something to be carried, and it has a new keeper: “When the tabernacle sets out, the Levites shall take it down, and when the tabernacle camps, the Levites shall set it up” (Num 1:51). Exodus does not give the building to the Levites. In Exodus the tabernacle is made by “all the skilled among those doing the work” (36:8), and “Moses set up the tabernacle” (40:18).

In Exodus the Levites stand beside the tabernacle in a single phrase. The accounts of its metals open, “These are the accounts of the tabernacle … counted at Moses’ word, the service of the Levites, by the hand of Ithamar son of Aaron the priest” (38:21). Numbers gives the carrying of the tabernacle to two families of Levites in the same words. Of the sons of Gershon, who carry its curtains, and of the sons of Merari, who carry its frames, it says that their service is “by the hand of Ithamar son of Aaron the priest” (Num 4:28, 33). The one phrase that ties the Levites to the tabernacle in Exodus is the phrase under which Numbers sets them to carry it.

So the tabernacle is put together at the end of Exodus, served through all of Leviticus, and made ready to travel in the first units of Numbers. Only then does the cloud lift, and the story that stopped at the mountain goes on.

Straight Down

The seven days of the priests are the loose end Part C left. In Unit 13, at the foot of the first column, they are commanded: “seven days you shall fill their hand” (29:35). In Unit 19 Moses is told again to bring Aaron and his sons near, wash them, dress them and anoint them (40:12–15). The setting up of the tabernacle is then told piece by piece, and the seven days are not told at all.

They are told in Leviticus Unit 4. “Moses brought Aaron and his sons near, and washed them with water” (Lev 8:6), dressed and anointed them, and kept them at the door of the tent, “for seven days he shall fill your hand” (Lev 8:33). “Aaron and his sons did all the things that YHWH commanded by the hand of Moses” (Lev 8:36). The foot of the first column of Exodus is an order, and the carrying out lies beneath it, in the next book.

Whether more than this runs down from Exodus depends on how Leviticus is laid out. The map of Leviticus, as the commentary on that book draws it, is in the table below, with Exodus above it and each unit’s short description. At its centre stands Leviticus Unit 13, which begins “You shall be holy.” Around it the book is built in rings, each a group of three units before the centre answered by a group of three after it, and each ring has a mark of its own. In the inner ring a person’s relatives are named again and again. In the middle ring the count is “seven days” and then “the eighth day” (Lev 8:33 and 9:1; 12:2–3; 22:27; 23:36). The outer ring says where the laws were given, at the tent of meeting and at Mount Sinai (Lev 1:1; 7:38; 25:1; 27:34). The three units of impurity belong to no ring: every other group of three is answered by a group on the other side of the centre, and these are answered by none. What they deal with, scale disease and the flows of the body, is what has to be removed before anyone may come near, and they stand before the inner ring as a screen (parokhet) stands before the holy of holies, and are set aside. The commentary on Leviticus gives the reasons in full, in Part B: The Map of Leviticus. In each group of three, one unit faces the deity, one faces the people, and one stands between; after the centre the groups are turned over, as the book turns there from the single person to the community. All of this comes from Leviticus read by itself.

Exodus has two pairs of long columns around its own middle, and Leviticus has two rings inside its outer one. Laid one under the other, middle to middle, the four long columns of Exodus fall on the inner ring and the middle ring of Leviticus, and the outer ring stands beyond Exodus at both ends. What is new here is only that one book is laid over the other.

The two books then fit by their parts as well as by their places. Part C read the four long columns of Exodus as four parts: the first and the last complete each other, and the two inner columns are the means by which the first becomes the last. The two means lie on the inner ring of Leviticus, the ring of the person and his relatives. The first and the last lie on the middle ring, the ring of the seven days and the eighth. The subjects agree. The means are how each of the people becomes YHWH’s and how they come to belong to one another, and the ring beneath them speaks of a man’s house: Aaron atones “for himself and for his house” (Lev 16:6), and the laws are given to “any man of the house of Israel” (Lev 17:3). The first and the last are YHWH with Moses and YHWH with the whole people, and in the ring beneath them YHWH is seen by all the people and made holy in their midst: “today YHWH appears to you” (Lev 9:4), and “I will be made holy in the midst of the children of Israel” (Lev 22:32).

The first column holds YHWH with Moses alone and the pattern shown to him; it is how things stand at the start. Beneath it, in Leviticus Units 4–6, the seven days are carried out, and on the eighth day the new priests make their first offerings. Fire comes out and consumes what is on the altar, “and the glory of YHWH appeared to all the people” (Lev 9:23–24).

Two of Aaron’s sons then bring fire that no one had commanded, and fire comes out a second time and consumes them (Lev 10:1–2). Moses tells Aaron what YHWH had said: “Through those near to me I will be made holy” (Lev 10:3). At the head of the column, the first words spoken at the bush were “Do not come near” (3:5), and Moses “hid his face, for he was afraid to look at the deity” (3:6). Here two men come near, and die of it.

Leviticus Unit 5 tells Israel to divide the animals it may eat from those it may not (Lev 11:47), “for I am YHWH who brings you up from the land of Egypt” (Lev 11:45); in Unit 3 the dividing had been done for them, “YHWH sets Egypt apart from Israel” (11:7), in another verb. Leviticus Unit 6 is the law of a woman who gives birth (Lev 12:2), and Unit 1 of Exodus begins with the midwives at the births of Israel’s children, and then tells of Moses’ own (1:15–19; 2:2). The first column of Exodus is the start of things, and so are the units beneath it.

The second column holds each of the people made YHWH’s, in danger of death, from the blood on the doors to the half shekel given “to atone for your lives” (30:15). Beneath it, in Leviticus Unit 10, Aaron goes behind the veil with blood, and the danger is named twice: he is to come in only so, “so that he does not die” (Lev 16:2, 13). He atones “for himself and for his house” three times, and the third time “all the assembly of Israel” is added (Lev 16:6, 11, 17). The day comes “once in the year” (Lev 16:34). Exodus has that phrase in one place, at the foot of this column (30:10), and Leviticus has it only here.

Leviticus Unit 11 turns from Aaron’s house to every man’s. “Any man of the house of Israel” who slaughters an ox, a sheep or a goat is to bring it to the door of the tent of meeting, where its blood is thrown on the altar (Lev 17:3–6). On the night of the going out the blood went on the door of each house. The unit says what the blood is for: “I have given it to you on the altar, to atone for your lives” (Lev 17:11). It is the half shekel’s phrase. In Exodus it is said only of the silver, and in Leviticus only of the blood. Leviticus Unit 12 then begins, “You shall not do as they do in the land of Egypt, where you lived” (Lev 18:3).

The middle column holds Moses and the whole people together, and at its centre, in Unit 10, Moses reads the book of the covenant and the people take on YHWH’s law. Beneath it is Leviticus Unit 13, the centre of that book: “Speak to all the congregation of the children of Israel and say to them, You shall be holy, for I YHWH your deity am holy” (Lev 19:2). It is a law for each of them, and it is spoken to all of them at once.

The law given there is both kinds of law that Part C found taken on at the middle of Exodus. One is the law of the people among themselves: “you shall do no wrong in judgment” (Lev 19:15, 35), just weights and measures (Lev 19:36), and the stranger, “for you were strangers in the land of Egypt” (Lev 19:34), in the words of the last laws (22:20 [English 22:21]; 23:9). The other is the ordered way of coming before YHWH: whoever eats the offering past its day “has profaned the holy thing of YHWH” (Lev 19:8), the guilt offering is brought “to the door of the tent of meeting” (Lev 19:21), and “you shall fear my sanctuary” (Lev 19:30). The unit ends, “You shall keep all my statutes and all my judgments (mishpatai)” (Lev 19:37), with the word that opened the cases of Exodus (21:1) and that Moses told the people at the covenant (24:3). Its laws stand in two columns, one personal and one communal.

In Exodus the people are to be holy as YHWH’s own: “you shall be to me a kingdom of priests and a holy nation” (19:6), “holy people you shall be to me” (22:30 [English 22:31]). Exodus never says “for I am holy.” At the centre of Leviticus the reason given is YHWH’s own holiness, and the next unit joins keeping the law to being made holy: “You shall keep my statutes and do them; I am YHWH who makes you holy” (Lev 20:8). At the middle of Exodus the people take on the law. At the middle of Leviticus, keeping it makes them holy as he is holy.

Among the words given with the second tablets is “Molten deities you shall not make for yourself” (34:17), and among the first commands of Leviticus Unit 13 is “Molten deities you shall not make for yourselves” (Lev 19:4). Neither book uses the phrase anywhere else.

The fourth column holds the people joined to one another, by the law that protects them from one another and by what each of them gives. Beneath it, Leviticus Units 14–16 give cases of their own, and say who is to carry them out. A man gives his child to Molech, and “the people of the land shall stone him” (Lev 20:2). One law is the same in both books: whoever curses his father or his mother shall be put to death (21:17; Lev 20:9). The gifts are there too. In Exodus every man and woman brought “a freewill gift to YHWH” for the tabernacle (35:29), and the same units of Leviticus give the rules for a freewill gift (Lev 22:18, 21).

The last column holds YHWH present with the whole people, and the house they built. Beneath it, Leviticus Units 17–19 give the nation its year, feast by feast (Lev 23:1–44), as the last laws gave it three feasts in the year (23:14).

The same units order the lamp and the bread. The table and the lampstand were made in Unit 17 (37:10, 17), and the plan had already commanded the bread “before me continually” and the lamp “continually” (25:30; 27:20). In the last unit of Exodus they are set out once, by Moses, on the day the tabernacle is set up (40:23, 25). Leviticus Unit 19 gives the standing service again: Aaron keeps the lamp from evening to morning, as the plan had said (27:21), and the bread is set out anew every Sabbath (Lev 24:3, 8).

In Leviticus Unit 19 a man curses the Name, and “all the congregation shall stone him” (Lev 24:14). At the head of the column, in Unit 7, the whole people stood at the foot of the mountain and were warned that whoever touched it would be stoned (19:13). The unit ends, “and the children of Israel did as YHWH commanded Moses” (Lev 24:23), and nearly the same words are heard in Unit 19 when the finished work is brought to Moses: “according to all that YHWH commanded Moses, so the children of Israel did all the work” (39:42).

The seven days are an order in one book and its carrying out in the next. Under the second column and the middle, each book has a phrase found in no other place, and the place in Leviticus lies directly beneath the place in Exodus. The other columns hold less. Even so, the map of Exodus does not appear to have been drawn to stop at the foot of the book.

The Same Turn

Part C read the map across, from Moses alone, by way of the law read at the middle, to all the house of Israel. Leviticus turns the same way. Its units before Leviticus Unit 13 speak mostly to one person at a time: someone who brings an offering, a priest, a woman who has given birth, Aaron alone behind the veil. Its units after Leviticus Unit 13 speak to the community. Laid one over the other, the two books turn in the same place. In Exodus the one and the many meet in a covenant, and directly beneath it the whole congregation is told, “You shall be holy, for I YHWH your deity am holy.”

The sentence that Part B found holding the whole book goes on as well. In Unit 14 it is given as YHWH’s promise: “I will dwell in the midst of the children of Israel, and I will be their deity … who brought them out of the land of Egypt” (29:45–46). In Leviticus Unit 17, on the far side of the centre, it comes back: “I will be made holy in the midst of the children of Israel. I am YHWH who makes you holy, who brings you out of the land of Egypt to be your deity” (Lev 22:32–33). In Exodus YHWH dwells in their midst. In Leviticus he is made holy there.

Where the Pattern Is Thin

The three rings of the camp are the plainest thing in this part, and one corner of them is soft. That the Levites take the tabernacle down and set it up is said in so many words only in Numbers. In Exodus it is made by the skilled and set up by Moses, and the Levites are tied to it only in the phrase of the accounts (38:21). That Numbers uses the same phrase for their carrying (Num 4:28, 33) joins the two books; it does not make the Levites builders. The sons of Levi appear once more in Exodus, with their swords after the calf (32:26–28), and that is another matter.

The matching of the columns starts from the two middles. It sets aside the impurity units of Leviticus and leaves its outer ring beyond Exodus. Each of these is a feature of Leviticus, found without Exodus in view, but the reading of the columns down into Leviticus rests on them, and if the map of Leviticus does not hold, nothing laid over it holds either. The same holds for the four parts lying on the two rings: they fit only if the books are matched as they are here.

The second column and the middle are the firm ones. Each has the column’s subject beneath it, and a phrase that the two books share nowhere else. The tie of the middle to Leviticus Unit 13 rests on more than that phrase, on the law taken on at the middle of Exodus and given at the middle of Leviticus, and that is a tie of subject, not of words. The words that Leviticus Unit 13 shares with Exodus are mostly those of the last laws, in Unit 9, in the last column; nothing in Leviticus Unit 13 is written or read, since it is all spoken to the congregation. “For I am holy” is also said in Leviticus Units 5, 14 and 15, so what is said of Leviticus Unit 13 rests on its place at the centre and on its being spoken to all the congregation, not on the phrase alone. The last column is nearly as firm in another way: it has no phrase of its own, but each of its four units has something beneath it.

The first column is the weakest. Its foot is carried out to the letter. For the rest, what Leviticus Units 4–6 share with it is that they are beginnings, which is wider than what Part C says of the column. They are not about Moses alone with the people elsewhere: the whole congregation is called to the door to watch (Lev 8:3–4). The birth is a tie of subject; the verb for bearing is the same in both (va-teled, 2:2; ve-yaledah, Lev 12:2), and the other words are not. The fourth column is fair. The road of Unit 6, with its bread and its quarrels, has nothing beneath it, and Leviticus Unit 15 is about the priests’ own families.

Some things cross from one column to another. “Eye for eye” is in the cases, in the fourth column (21:24), and comes back in Leviticus Unit 19, beneath the last (Lev 24:20). The lamp and the bread are commanded “continually” in Unit 11, in the first column (25:30; 27:20), and their standing service is given in Leviticus Unit 19, beneath the last (Lev 24:2–8). The sentence about dwelling in their midst does not keep to one column either. In Exodus it is at the foot of the second; in Leviticus it is beneath the last, and it comes once more in the outer ring, with both its halves together: “I will set my dwelling in your midst … and I will be your deity, and you shall be my people” (Lev 26:11–12). The seventh year of the land is out there too (Lev 25:2–4), where Exodus has it in the last laws (23:10–11). “The children of Israel did as YHWH commanded Moses,” set beneath the last column, is said in nearly the same words on the night of the going out (12:28, 50), in the second. So the groups we compared do not hold everything Leviticus takes up from Exodus, and its last units gather some of it again.

The turn from one to many would be there however the columns were matched. It is something the two books share, and it does not show that the matching is right.

What Comes Next

Exodus ends with a house built and filled with glory, a people gathered around it that has not yet moved, and one order from the mountain still waiting, the seven days of the priests. Leviticus takes that order up, and the tabernacle finished in the last unit of Exodus becomes the place around which a whole people’s life is ordered and made holy, and in Numbers the centre of a camp on the march. In Exodus YHWH comes to dwell in their midst; what that asks of them is the next book’s business.

From here the units are taken one at a time, beginning at the beginning, with a family that came down to Egypt and a king who did not know Joseph.
