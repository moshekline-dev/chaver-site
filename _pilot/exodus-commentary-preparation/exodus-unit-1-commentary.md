# Exodus Unit 1: A People and Their Deliverer

*Exodus 1:1–4:18 · First draft*

*Provisional unit commentary, to follow [the four-part introduction to Exodus](exodus-four-part-overview.md).*

## The Unit within the Book

The first unit opens the group devoted to deliverance from Egypt, Units 1–4. Its account of Moses’ preparation begins a development of leadership that continues through Moses and Aaron’s public mission and into the wilderness and Sinai group, Units 6–9. At the other end of the book, the people’s willing contributions and skilled work for the dwelling will answer the opening picture of labor imposed by Pharaoh. The immediate question is how Israel’s suffering and Moses’ separate history become one mission; the larger question is what kind of shared life that mission will make possible.

## Who Made You a Ruler?

Moses sees an Egyptian striking a Hebrew and intervenes. The following day he finds two Hebrews fighting and intervenes again. This time he receives a question: “Who made you a ruler and a judge over us?” (2:14). The question exposes something missing from his first attempt to help his brothers. He has recognized their suffering, but they have not recognized his authority. His intervention ends with his flight from Egypt.

At the burning bush, the question returns in another form. Told to bring Israel out of Egypt, Moses asks, “Who am I, that I should go to Pharaoh?” (3:11). Earlier, a Hebrew challenged his right to act. Now Moses challenges his own ability to act. Between these two questions lies the movement of the first unit of Exodus: the man who acts without being sent must become the man who can act because he has been sent.

But this is only one side of the composition. The opening chapters tell two histories, that of Israel and that of Moses. The people multiply, suffer oppression and survive a campaign against their children. Moses is born, encounters oppression and survives Pharaoh’s attempt to kill him. These histories develop separately until the divine response to Israel’s suffering becomes a call addressed to Moses.

The arrangement allows us to follow both histories at once. It also shows why neither history is sufficient by itself. Israel needs a deliverer, while Moses needs a people to whom he can return and an authority under which he can act.

## The Unit’s Architecture

The unit contains three rows and two columns. Column A follows Israel; Column B follows Moses. The first row presents their histories, the middle row brings each into relation with divine attention, and the final row joins the plan for Israel to the preparation of its messenger.

| Row | Column A: Israel | Column B: Moses |
|---|---|---|
| **1 · History** | **1A · 1:1–22:** Growth, oppression and the preservation of Israel’s children | **1B · 2:1–22:** Birth, intervention and the establishment of Moses’ household in Midian |
| **2 · Divine encounter** | **2A · 2:23–25:** Israel cries out; Elohim hears, remembers, sees and knows | **2B · 3:1–5:** Moses turns to see; YHWH sees him turn, and Elohim calls |
| **3 · The mission** | **3A · 3:6–22:** The promise of deliverance and the message for Israel | **3B · 4:1–18:** Signs, speech and assistance prepare Moses to return |

We read the biblical sequence through 1A, then 1B, then 2A and 2B, and finally 3A and 3B. Reading across a row brings corresponding stages together; reading down a column follows the development of the people or their deliverer. The columns identify centers of attention, not exclusive casts of characters. Moses must appear in the plan for Israel, just as Israel must appear in the preparation of Moses.

The boundaries follow this movement. The opening names reconnect us with Jacob’s household in Genesis. At the end, Moses asks leave to return to his brothers, and Jethro answers, “Go in peace” (4:18). The next verse renews the divine command to return, and 4:20 narrates the departure with his family. Our unit therefore ends with readiness to leave Midian, before the return journey and the public testing of the commission.

The two outer rows have internal subdivisions. Row 1 contains three pairs; Row 3 contains four. The brief middle row brings the two histories into the encounter that makes their common future possible.

## Two Histories: Row 1

The first pair begins with a household. Israel’s ancestors enter Egypt, “each man and his household,” *ish uveito* (1:1). Opposite this stands “a man from the house of Levi,” *ish mibbeit Levi* (2:1). The national history and the individual history begin in the same social setting, but at different scales.

Their growth is also paired. “The children of Israel were fruitful and swarmed and multiplied” (1:7); “the child grew” (2:10). The first statement looks back toward Genesis. Fruitfulness, multiplication and the filling of the land recall the language of creation, while the list of names recalls the family that entered Egypt. What was a household has become a people. The corresponding passage follows a single child until he is brought into another household and given a name.

Moses’ survival makes the national danger personal. Pharaoh has ordered that sons be cast into the Nile. The mother places her son at its edge, but within a protective *tevah*. The word recalls Noah’s vessel: a life that water threatens to destroy is preserved in an enclosure upon that water. Here, however, the rescue is carried forward through the actions of the mother, sister and Pharaoh’s daughter. No divine speech instructs them in this scene.

Pharaoh’s distinction between sons and daughters also turns against his purpose. Daughters are to live; his own daughter preserves a son. She recognizes him as a Hebrew child before taking responsibility for him (2:6). Her compassion crosses the boundary her father’s policy is intended to enforce.

The second pair takes us from growth to conflict:

| | Israel: 1Ab · 1:8–14 | Moses: 1Bb · 2:11–15 |
|---|---|---|
| **Burdens** | Taskmasters afflict Israel “with their burdens,” *besivlotam* | Moses goes out and sees “their burdens,” *besivlotam* |
| **Authority** | Officials, *sarei missim*, are set over the people | Moses is challenged: “Who made you a ruler,” *sar*, “and a judge over us?” |
| **Violence** | Pharaoh fears that Israel will fight against Egypt | Moses strikes the Egyptian who is striking a Hebrew |

The repeated “burdens” joins the national account to Moses’ experience. What Column A describes as a condition of the people, Column B presents as something an individual sees and answers. The difference is consequential. Pharaoh fears the people’s power and organizes coercion; Moses sees their suffering and attempts protection. Yet the attempt does not establish him as their leader. Pharaoh seeks to kill him, and he leaves the land that the Israelites remain unable to leave.

In the third pair, the preservation of life leads toward the continuation of households. Shiphrah and Puah refuse the king’s command, preserve the boys and receive divine favor. Across from them, Moses protects the priest of Midian’s daughters, receives a place in their father’s household, marries Zipporah and fathers a son.

The repeated sons and daughters belong to the argument. Pharaoh treats birth as something to control; the midwives preserve it. Moses, himself preserved by women, now protects women and becomes a father. His rescue of the daughters is described with the verb “deliver”: “An Egyptian delivered us from the hand of the shepherds” (2:19). Shortly afterward, YHWH will announce the intention to “deliver” Israel “from the hand of Egypt” (3:8). Moses has already performed, on a small scale, the kind of action for which he will be commissioned.

There is an unresolved difficulty in the daughters’ report. They identify him as “an Egyptian.” Moses has gone out to his Hebrew brothers, but in Midian he is recognized by his Egyptian identity. His son’s name, Gershom, gives voice to his position: “I have been a sojourner in a foreign land” (2:22). Row 1 has brought him into a household without resolving where he belongs.

The row thus develops two related histories rather than two identical ones. Israel’s growth continues under oppression; Moses’ growth carries him out of Egypt. The people remain in need of deliverance, while the potential deliverer has become a stranger elsewhere.

## Hearing and Seeing: Row 2

The middle row changes the perspective. Until now, we have followed the actions of kings, midwives, mothers, daughters and Moses. Elohim has favored the midwives, but Moses has received no divine commission. Now the narrator brings Israel’s suffering and Moses’ attention into a paired divine encounter.

In 2A, Israel’s cry rises to haElohim. The following clauses name Elohim repeatedly: Elohim hears their groaning, remembers the covenant, sees the children of Israel and knows (2:24–25). The Hebrew ends simply with “Elohim knew,” leaving the object unstated. The sequence does not yet announce what will happen. It establishes that the suffering is heard and seen within the continuing relationship with Abraham, Isaac and Jacob.

Across from this, Moses sees a bush burning without being consumed. He turns aside to examine it. YHWH sees that he has turned, and Elohim calls to him from the bush (3:4).

The directions of communication differ. In Egypt, human distress rises toward the divine; at Horeb, a divine call reaches the human observer. Seeing joins the two scenes. Elohim sees Israel; YHWH sees Moses turn to see. The suffering people and the attentive individual have entered one field of divine concern, although they remain geographically separated.

The divine names mark this transition with particular precision. YHWH first appears in Exodus in the phrase “the angel of YHWH” (3:2). Two verses later, the narrator places the names within one sequence: YHWH sees, Elohim calls. We should preserve that sequence rather than replace both names with an undifferentiated “God.” The text is bringing the names together at the moment when the history of the people becomes a personal summons.

The encounter also introduces holy ground. Moses is told both that he has been addressed and that he cannot simply approach: “Do not draw near here”; remove your sandals (3:5). Contact requires recognition of a boundary. This is the first instruction he receives at the bush, before the account of the mission. The person who previously acted on his own initiative must now learn how to respond to an address that also sets limits.

Row 2 is therefore a middle in more than location. It looks back to the histories of suffering and displacement, and forward to the mission that will join them. It preserves the distinction between Israel and Moses while establishing the relation through which their futures can meet.

## Promise and Preparation: Row 3

The last row unfolds as four pairs:

| Pair | Column A: The plan for Israel | Column B: Preparing Moses |
|---|---|---|
| **a · Signs** | **3:6–12:** Deliverance promised; service on this mountain given as a sign | **4:1–9:** Signs supplied to address Israel’s possible disbelief |
| **b · Words** | **3:13–15:** What Moses is to say about the one who sends him | **4:10–12:** How Moses, who protests his inability to speak, will speak |
| **c · Representation** | **3:16–21a:** The elders, the approach to Pharaoh and YHWH’s intervention | **4:13–17:** Aaron will accompany Moses’ mission as his spokesman |
| **d · Departure** | **3:21b–22:** Israel’s departure will include goods for sons and daughters | **4:18:** Moses obtains leave to return to his brothers |

The division within 3:21 is important. “I will give this people favor in the eyes of the Egyptians” completes 3Ac. “When you go, you shall not go empty” begins 3Ad and leads into the silver, gold and garments of 3:22. The subdivisions follow the movement of the argument within the verse.

### A Sign for the People, Signs for the Messenger

YHWH’s first declaration takes up the language of the middle row: “I have surely seen” the affliction, “I have heard” the cry, “I know” the suffering (3:7). What the narrator reported of Elohim now becomes YHWH’s first-person explanation to Moses. The names are related through shared perception and a common undertaking.

The declaration moves from “I have come down to deliver them” (3:8) to “I will send you” (3:10). The descent of the divine and the sending of a human are presented as connected actions. Moses’ question, “Who am I?”, receives the answer “I will be with you,” *ehyeh immakh* (3:12). His own standing is answered by promised accompaniment.

The paired signs serve different needs. The sign in 3Aa points forward: the delivered people will serve haElohim on this mountain. The signs in 3Ba address a difficulty before departure: how will the people believe that YHWH has appeared to Moses? The staff, the hand and the promised transformation of Nile water provide means of recognition. One side describes the destination of the mission; the other prepares its messenger to be received.

These signs also return to Moses’ earlier experience. He fled Pharaoh; now he flees the serpent before being told to grasp it. The Nile from which he was drawn becomes the source of water that will turn to blood on dry ground. The new commission takes up elements of the history we have already read, but places their use under divine instruction.

### What to Say, and How to Speak

The next pair concerns speech. In 3Ab, Moses asks what he should say if Israel asks the name of the one who sent him. In 3Bb, he protests that he is not a man of words. The first difficulty concerns the message; the second concerns the messenger.

The opening formulas make the correspondence visible. “Moses said to haElohim” introduces the question about the name (3:13). “Moses said to YHWH” introduces the objection about speech (4:10). Between these statements, the name has been communicated. The narrator now identifies the addressee as YHWH; Moses’ own opening address in 4:10 is *Adonai*. The change in the narrative formula is significant without attributing the narrator’s words to Moses.

The phrase *ehyeh asher ehyeh* (3:14) belongs within this exchange. Before it appears, Moses has heard *ehyeh immakh*, “I will be with you.” After it, he is promised, “I will be with your mouth,” *ehyeh im pikha* (4:12). Whatever further meaning we find in the self-designation, its immediate setting concerns presence: the one who sends Moses will accompany him, including his speaking. The response to his inadequacy develops from the general promise of accompaniment into a promise directed toward the particular faculty he distrusts.

### Who Will Speak for Whom?

In 3Ac, Moses is told to gather the elders. They are to hear his report and accompany him to the king. Israel will be represented before Pharaoh, while YHWH will intervene through an outstretched hand.

In 3Bc, Moses asks that someone else be sent. The provision of Aaron answers the need for a spokesman without removing Moses from the mission. Moses will put the words in Aaron’s mouth; Aaron will be his mouth to the people; Moses will be “as Elohim” to Aaron (4:16).

Here the text itself supplies a relation through which to understand representation. Moses receives words and passes them to someone who will speak them. His difficulty does not disappear through a declaration that he is secretly eloquent. It is answered through assistance and an ordered relationship between speakers. The man whose authority was challenged in Egypt is being given a role that he did not confer upon himself.

### Leaving and Returning

The final pair turns preparation toward movement. Israel’s promised departure reverses the conditions of the opening row. Pharaoh sought to destroy sons while preserving daughters; now both sons and daughters are to receive the goods taken from Egypt (3:22). The children whose lives were treated as a royal possession appear as beneficiaries of the departure.

Across from this promise stands a much smaller act. Moses asks Jethro for permission to return and see whether his brothers are still alive. He does not recount the revelation in this request. The narrator lets the immense promise of national deliverance stand beside the ordinary leave-taking through which Moses begins to respond.

His word “brothers” returns us to the earlier intervention. He first went out to his brothers and saw their burdens (2:11). Having fled, married and named himself a sojourner through Gershom’s name, he now asks to return to those same brothers. The personal history has not been discarded. It has become the history through which the commission can be undertaken.

## Reading the Unit

“Who made you a ruler and a judge over us?” is not answered when Moses strikes the Egyptian. It is answered through the remainder of the unit. His concern for his brothers, his resistance to violence and his ability to deliver others are already present. What he lacks is a recognized mission and the means to communicate it. The encounter at Horeb supplies an authority, a message, signs and a spokesman; the ending supplies his first movement toward return.

The two columns show why the call belongs with the opening history of oppression. Israel’s cry and Moses’ turning aside occupy corresponding positions in the middle row. From that point, the promise for the people and the preparation of the individual develop together. Deliverance requires their connection.

The unit begins with a family that entered Egypt and ends with a man preparing to reenter it. Between these movements, the family becomes a people, the child becomes a prospective deliverer, and the Elohim who remembers the ancestors is encountered in the summons and name of YHWH. Nothing has yet changed in Pharaoh’s administration. Israel is still enslaved. But the histories that had separated the people from Moses now converge in a commission to bring them out.
